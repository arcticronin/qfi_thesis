\chapter{The Exoplanet Problem, Theory}
\label{ch:astrophysical_theory}

This chapter establishes the theoretical basis for applying quantum parameter estimation to high-contrast astronomical imaging.  The problem is to estimate the separation between a bright star and a much fainter exoplanet when their images overlap below the Rayleigh limit.  In this regime, the attainable precision depends on both the quantum state of the weak thermal light and the measurement used to detect it~\cite{Tsang2016quantum}.

The source model and the spatial-mode demultiplexing (SPADE) receiver presented here are based mainly on the established framework of Refs.~\cite{Santamaria2024single,Tsang2016quantum}.  Direct imaging measures the position of each detected photon, while SPADE projects the light onto spatial modes matched to the telescope's point-spread function (PSF).  We use a star-centred coordinate convention and assume that the mode sorter is ideally aligned with the star.  This makes it possible to show how a small planetary displacement appears in the first-order Hermite--Gaussian modes.

The work of this chapter is to express this optical model consistently in the density-operator, measurement, and Fisher-information notation introduced earlier in the thesis.  It also makes explicit the assumptions needed to connect the ideal source model to the available experimental observables.  The original experimental analysis is developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:exoplanet_experiment}.

\section{The exoplanet characterization problem}
Consider a star, denoted by $A$, with intensity $I_A$, and an exoplanet, denoted by $B$, with intensity $I_B$. This system is characterized by the relative intensity ratio (or contrast ratio) $\epsilon$
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon \coloneqq \frac{I_B}{I_A+I_B}
    \label{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon}
\end{equation}
Since the exoplanet is typically much fainter than its host star, so that $I_B \ll I_A$, $\epsilon$ can be approximated as ~\cite{Santamaria2024single}
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon \approx \frac{I_B}{I_A}
    \label{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon_approx}
\end{equation}
The approximation defined in Eq. \ref{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon_approx} is the one used throughout the whole thesis.

\section{The sub-Rayleigh regime}
% ==============================================================================
When the angular separation between the star and the planet is small, their light fields overlap at the telescope's aperture, making the faint planet difficult to distinguish from the much brighter star. Detecting and characterizing the planet therefore requires resolving the two sources at sub-Rayleigh scales, i.e., separations smaller than the classic Rayleigh diffraction limit.
This limit arises because, due to diffraction at the telescope's finite aperture, light from a point source cannot be focused to an infinitesimal point. Instead, the telescope produces a finite intensity distribution.
This distribution is the result of the incoming image and the telescope's own response, quantified by a function known as the point-spread function (PSF). The PSF is a property of the instrument, not of the source. For point-like sources, such as those considered here, the observed image is described directly by the PSF itself.
The PSF is commonly modeled as a Gaussian of width $w_0$ ~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The Rayleigh limit is then reached when the transverse separation $d$ between the two emitters becomes comparable to or smaller than $w_0$ ($d\lesssim w_0$)~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This transition from the resolved to the sub-Rayleigh regime is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:rayleigh_criterion}, where the increasing overlap between the two PSFs causes the distinct intensity peaks to merge into a single dominant profile.

\section{Why an intensity image is poorly conditioned}
Direct imaging is a common astronomical technique for detecting exoplanets. It involves capturing photographs or light spectra of an exoplanet by pointing a telescope directly at it.

Unfortunately, this detection method is not suitable for the sub-Rayleigh regime we want to treat in this thesis. To address this point more formally, below we compute the Fisher information for the system under study.

We consider the exoplanet and the star separated by a distance $d$ along the $x$ direction, with the star's position taken as known, and we model the PSF as a Gaussian of width $w_0$. Direct imaging records the transverse position at which each detected photon lands on the image plane. Conditional on a photon being detected, this position is distributed according to
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{DI}}(x,y\mid d,\epsilon)
    = \frac{e^{-y^2/(2w_0^2)}}{2\pi w_0^2}
    \left[(1-\epsilon)e^{-x^2/(2w_0^2)}
        +\epsilon e^{-(x-d)^2/(2w_0^2)}\right],
    \label{eq:di_probability_distribution}
\end{equation}
Note that Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution} describes only the \emph{spatial} distribution of a detected photon; it says nothing about whether a photon is detected at all in a given temporal mode. Here, a temporal mode can be thought of simply as one elementary time slot into which the incoming light is divided, short enough that at most one photon is expected in it; the probability that this slot actually yields a detected photon is denoted $\eta$, introduced below. In other words, direct imaging is fundamentally a position-basis measurement of the field: it reads out the intensity pattern pixel by pixel.

%This is in contrast to SPADE, discussed in the following sections, which instead projects the field onto a different, non-local set of spatial modes before the photon is counted.

Equation~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution} already makes clear the difficulty of direct imaging in the sub-Rayleigh regime: when $d\ll w_0$, the two Gaussian terms in brackets are nearly identical, so shifting the faint source by $d$ barely deforms the overall image; on top of this weak effect, the deformation itself enters only through the small prefactor $\epsilon$, since the companion contributes just a fraction $\epsilon$ of the detected photons. Both effects work in the same direction, and their combination is what ultimately suppresses the information about $d$ carried by the image.

This intuition is made quantitative by computing the Fisher information for the estimation of $d$, treating the star's position and the contrast $\epsilon$ as known. In the high-contrast, sub-Rayleigh limit, the Fisher information per temporal mode behaves as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{DI}}(d) \sim \frac{\eta\epsilon^2}{w_0^2},
    \qquad d\ll w_0,
    \label{eq:di_fisher_collapse}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is, as above, the probability that a given temporal mode yields a detected photon. The key feature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_fisher_collapse} is the quadratic dependence on $\epsilon$: the displacement signal is suppressed twice over, once because the companion is faint to begin with, and a second time because its already-weak imprint on the image must be estimated against the much brighter stellar background. This double suppression is precisely the limitation that motivates moving to a different measurement basis~\cite{Santamaria2024single,Tsang2016quantum}. In the following section, we show how the same photons can be measured in a different spatial basis, which is better suited to the sub-Rayleigh regime.

\section{Weak astronomical light as a spatial quantum state}

The probability distribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution} can be recast as a quantum description of the transverse spatial degree of freedom of the collected light.  To do so, the observation time is divided into temporal modes short enough that the mean photon number in each mode is much smaller than one.  With probability $1-\eta$ no photon is detected and the mode is in the vacuum state $\ket{0}$; with probability $\eta$ one photon is detected.  Neglecting the much less likely multiphoton events, the state of one temporal mode is therefore~\cite{Santamaria2024single}
\begin{equation}
    \rho_d=(1-\eta)|0\rangle\langle0|
    +\eta(1-\epsilon)|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|
    +\eta\epsilon|\psi_d\rangle\langle\psi_d|
    \label{eq:optical_density_matrix_chap}
\end{equation}
Here $|\psi_0\rangle$ and $|\psi_d\rangle$ are the normalized one-photon spatial states associated with the star and the planet, respectively.  The weights of the three alternatives sum to one: $1-\eta$ is the probability of no detection, whereas $\eta(1-\epsilon)$ and $\eta\epsilon$ are the probabilities of detecting a photon originating from the star and the planet.  Equation~\eqref{eq:optical_density_matrix_chap} is a statistical mixture, not a coherent superposition.  This distinction expresses the mutual incoherence of the two astronomical sources: there is no stable relative phase between their fields and hence no interference term between $|\psi_0\rangle$ and $|\psi_d\rangle$.

The spatial wavefunction is the probability amplitude for detecting the photon at position $(x,y)$.  Consistently with the Gaussian PSF introduced above, and choosing the star as the origin, the two amplitudes are
\begin{align}
    \langle x,y|\psi_0\rangle
     & =\left(\frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2}\right)^{1/2}
    \exp\!\left[-\frac{x^2+y^2}{4w_0^2}\right],
    \label{eq:star_wavefunction}                \\
    \langle x,y|\psi_d\rangle
     & =\left(\frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2}\right)^{1/2}
    \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-d_x)^2+(y-d_y)^2}{4w_0^2}\right].
    \label{eq:planet_wavefunction}
\end{align}
where $\boldsymbol d=(d_x,d_y)$ is the planet's transverse displacement from the star and $d=\sqrt{d_x^2+d_y^2}$.  The factor of $4w_0^2$ in the amplitudes becomes $2w_0^2$ after taking the modulus squared, so $|\langle x,y|\psi_0\rangle|^2$ and $|\langle x,y|\psi_d\rangle|^2$ reproduce the Gaussian intensity profiles used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution}.

Conditioned on the detection of one photon, the vacuum term is removed and the spatial state becomes
\begin{equation}
    \rho_d^{(1)}=(1-\epsilon)|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|
    +\epsilon|\psi_d\rangle\langle\psi_d|.
    \label{eq:conditional_one_photon_state}
\end{equation}
Direct imaging measures this state in the position basis: indeed,
$p_{\mathrm{DI}}(x,y\mid d,\epsilon)=\langle x,y|\rho_d^{(1)}|x,y\rangle$ (with $d_y=0$ in the one-dimensional geometry used above).  A different receiver can instead project the same photon onto another spatial basis.  Thus, the density-operator formalism does not imply that the astronomical field is deliberately prepared as a laboratory quantum state; it provides a compact statistical description of the spatial information carried by each collected photon and makes the measurement basis an explicit part of the estimation problem.

\section{SPADE: measuring in spatial modes}

The density-operator description above separates the state carried by the incoming light from the measurement performed on it. Direct imaging measures each detected photon in the position basis $\{|x,y\rangle\}$ and therefore records the intensity distribution pixel by pixel. The poor performance found in the sub-Rayleigh regime is therefore a limitation of this measurement strategy, rather than evidence that the collected light contains no information about the separation. This observation motivates looking for a measurement that uses the same detected photons more efficiently.

Spatial-mode demultiplexing (SPADE) provides such an alternative under ideal conditions. A SPADE receiver consists of a spatial-mode sorter followed by photon-counting detectors and the associated readout system. The mode sorter is the optical component that decomposes the transverse field into orthogonal spatial modes and directs each mode to a separate output port, where its photon counts are recorded~\cite{Tsang2016quantum,Santamaria2024single}. Thus, instead of determining where each photon lands, the receiver determines which spatial mode the photon occupies. Because these modal projections combine field amplitudes from different image-plane positions, they can access displacement information that is poorly represented in an unresolved intensity image. For a Gaussian PSF, the natural modes for this purpose are the Hermite--Gaussian (HG) modes.

\subsection{The Hermite--Gaussian spatial basis}

\begin{definition}[Hermite--Gaussian spatial basis]
    The transverse spatial state of one detected photon is described by a square-integrable complex wavefunction of the coordinates $(x,y)$.  The Hermite--Gaussian modes form a complete orthonormal basis of this infinite-dimensional spatial Hilbert space:
    \begin{equation}
        \langle HG_{mn}|HG_{pq}\rangle=\delta_{mp}\delta_{nq},
        \qquad
        |\psi\rangle=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}c_{mn}|HG_{mn}\rangle,
        \qquad
        c_{mn}=\langle HG_{mn}|\psi\rangle
        \label{eq:hg_complete_basis}
    \end{equation}
    Here $m$ and $n$ are non-negative integers that label the modal orders in the two transverse directions.  Each basis function consists of a Gaussian envelope multiplied by Hermite polynomials.  Increasing either index changes the transverse structure of the one-photon state; it does not add another photon.
\end{definition}

To describe the lowest-order modes ($m,n=0,1$), let $(x,y)$ denote transverse coordinates measured from the centre of the HG basis (these are the coordinates measured by the telescope).  In the index convention used in Ref.~\cite{Santamaria2024single}, $HG_{01}$ varies along the first coordinate and $HG_{10}$ along the second.\footnote{The ordering of the two HG indices is not universal.  This thesis follows Ref.~\cite{Santamaria2024single}, in which $HG_{01}$ is associated with the $x$ direction and $HG_{10}$ with the $y$ direction.  Other sources adopt the opposite ordering, for example the Photonics Encyclopedia~\cite{paschotta_hermite_gaussian} assigns the first index to $x$ and the second to $y$, so that the corresponding labels are exchanged.}
The three relevant normalized amplitudes are
\begin{align}
    \langle x,y|HG_{00}\rangle
     & =\left(\frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2}\right)^{1/2}
    \exp\!\left[-\frac{x^2+y^2}{4w_0^2}\right]
    \eqqcolon \psi_0(x,y) ,
    \label{eq:hg00_def}                         \\
    \langle x,y|HG_{01}\rangle
     & =\frac{x}{w_0}\psi_0(x,y),
    \label{eq:hg01_def}                         \\
    \langle x,y|HG_{10}\rangle
     & =\frac{y}{w_0}\psi_0(x,y).
    \label{eq:hg10_def}
\end{align}
The fundamental mode $HG_{00}$ is Gaussian.  Each first-order mode has one sign change in its field amplitude along one transverse direction.  Although the corresponding intensity remains non-negative, this sign change is important because the sorter acts on field amplitudes before the photons are counted.  Equations~\eqref{eq:hg00_def}--\eqref{eq:hg10_def} are the position-space version of the one-photon mode definitions in Ref.~\cite{Santamaria2024single}; they avoid the creation-operator notation used there, which is not needed for the present discussion.

\subsection{Alignment and SPADE inference}

The HG basis specifies a family of spatial profiles, but it does not by itself determine where the centre of the mode sorter lies relative to the sources.  In the coordinates $(x,y)$ introduced above, the star is at the origin and the planet is at $\boldsymbol d=(d_x,d_y)$. If the star is not perfectly centred on the sorter, the measured amplitudes are displaced relative to the HG basis.  This misalignment can be described by a vector $\bmdelta=(\delta_x,\delta_y)$ that locates the centre of the sorter relative to the star.  The local HG coordinates are therefore $x-\delta_x$ and $y-\delta_y$.  Perfect alignment with the star corresponds to $\bmdelta=\boldsymbol 0$.
Figure~\ref{fig:hg_modes} shows the fundamental and first-order HG amplitudes and intensities for an illustrative nonzero vertical offset.  It makes both the sign changes of the first-order amplitudes and the spatial reference defined by the sorter centre explicit.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/scripts_for_figures/hg_modes_visualization.png}
    \caption{The fundamental and first-order Hermite--Gaussian spatial modes for an illustrative vertical sorter offset, $\delta_x=0$ and $\delta_y=0.3$.  The first-order amplitudes change sign across one transverse axis, whereas their intensities remain non-negative.  Perfect alignment with the star corresponds instead to $\delta_x=\delta_y=0$.}
    \label{fig:hg_modes}
\end{figure}

The fundamental-port measurement is represented by the projector
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_0=|HG_{00}\rangle\langle HG_{00}|.
    \label{eq:pi0_def}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the probability $p_0$ of a count in this port is the projection of $\rho_d$ onto $HG_{00}$.  Following Eqs.~(7) and (8) of Ref.~\cite{Santamaria2024single},
\begin{align}
    p_0
     & \coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_d\Pi_0]
    =\langle HG_{00}|\rho_d|HG_{00}\rangle \notag \\
     & =\eta(1-\epsilon)
    e^{-(\delta_x^2+\delta_y^2)/(4w_0^2)}
    +\eta\epsilon
    e^{-[(d_x-\delta_x)^2+(d_y-\delta_y)^2]/(4w_0^2)}.
    \label{eq:p0_alignment}
\end{align}
The first term is the stellar contribution and the second is the planetary contribution.  Because the star dominates in the high-contrast regime, maximizing $p_0$ centres the sorter on the star.  This alignment is essential: a residual offset allows the bright stellar field to leak into the displacement-sensitive ports and can obscure the much weaker planetary signal. Here we assume ideal alignment, $\bmdelta=\boldsymbol 0$.

Under ideal alignment, the fundamental HG mode coincides with the stellar spatial state.  Expanding the displaced planetary Gaussian in powers of $d/w_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
    |\psi_{\boldsymbol d}\rangle
    \simeq |HG_{00}\rangle
    +\frac{d_x}{2w_0}|HG_{01}\rangle
    +\frac{d_y}{2w_0}|HG_{10}\rangle
    +\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{w_0^2}\right),
    \qquad d\ll w_0.
    \label{eq:hg_small_displacement_expansion}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:hg_small_displacement_expansion} shows that a small planetary displacement transfers amplitude from the fundamental mode to the two first-order modes.  Higher-order contributions enter at progressively higher powers of $d/w_0$, so the lowest-order modes carry the leading displacement response in the sub-Rayleigh regime even though the complete HG basis is infinite.

To connect this measurement with the reduced-space QFI analysis developed later in the thesis, we define the combined first-order projector
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_1=|HG_{01}\rangle\langle HG_{01}|
    +|HG_{10}\rangle\langle HG_{10}|.
    \label{eq:pi1_def}
\end{equation}
The low-order modal cutoff is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{\leq1}\coloneqq\Pi_0+\Pi_1,
    \qquad
    \Pi_{\mathrm{rest}}\coloneqq\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\leq1}.
    \label{eq:hg_low_order_cutoff}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:pi0_def}, \eqref{eq:pi1_def}, and \eqref{eq:hg_low_order_cutoff} retain the three lowest modes---one fundamental and two first-order modes---and collect all higher orders in the complementary outcome $\Pi_{\mathrm{rest}}$.  This is a fixed measurement subspace selected by the receiver, not a truncation based on the eigenvalues of $\rho_d$.  The distinction becomes important when SPADE is related to truncated QFI in the next chapter.

The individual first-order probabilities are the projections of $\rho_d$ onto $HG_{01}$ and $HG_{10}$.  Specializing Eqs.~(12) and (13) of Ref.~\cite{Santamaria2024single} to perfect alignment gives
\begin{align}
    p_{01}
     & \coloneqq\langle HG_{01}|\rho_d|HG_{01}\rangle
    =\eta\epsilon\frac{d_x^2}{4w_0^2}
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{4w_0^2}\right),
    \label{eq:p01_ideal}                              \\
    p_{10}
     & \coloneqq\langle HG_{10}|\rho_d|HG_{10}\rangle
    =\eta\epsilon\frac{d_y^2}{4w_0^2}
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{4w_0^2}\right).
    \label{eq:p10_ideal}
\end{align}
The probability of a count in either first-order port is their sum,
\begin{equation}
    p_1\coloneqq p_{01}+p_{10}
    =\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_d\Pi_1]
    =\eta\epsilon\frac{d_x^2+d_y^2}{4w_0^2}
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{4w_0^2}\right)
    \simeq\eta\epsilon\frac{d^2}{4w_0^2},
    \qquad d\ll w_0.
    \label{eq:p1_asymmetric}
\end{equation}
The factor $\eta\epsilon$ reflects that a first-order count requires both a detection and a photon from the faint source, while the quadratic factor $d^2/(4w_0^2)$ is the leading probability transferred by the displacement.  At leading order, $HG_{01}$ carries the $d_x^2$ contribution and $HG_{10}$ carries the $d_y^2$ contribution; their sum depends only on $d^2=d_x^2+d_y^2$.  First-order counts therefore determine the magnitude of the displacement but not its sign along either axis.  Recovering the sign would require an additional phase-sensitive measurement or an appropriate spatial reference.

Experimentally, the observed counts $n_0$, $n_{01}$, and $n_{10}$, or their relative frequencies, are compared with a calibrated forward model for $p_0$, $p_{01}$, and $p_{10}$.  The unknown parameter (which can be either $d$ or $\epsilon$) is then obtained by maximizing the corresponding likelihood, or equivalently by inverting a calibrated probability relation when only one parameter is unknown. At leading order, the impossibility to determine $d$ and $\epsilon$ separately is caused by the fact that $p_1$ depends on the product $\epsilon d^2$.  A joint estimate would require additional independent outcomes, measurement settings, or prior calibration.


\section{Takeaway}

High-contrast sub-Rayleigh imaging hides the faint planet's displacement within the much brighter stellar image.  Direct imaging measures this small deformation in the position basis and is therefore poorly conditioned.  SPADE instead resolves the same photons in a PSF-matched spatial basis: the star remains concentrated in the fundamental mode, while the displaced planet populates the first-order modes. SPADE does not remove diffraction or increase the available photon number. Its advantage is that it accesses spatial-mode statistics that are poorly represented in a raw intensity image.  Whether this advantage survives in a practical receiver depends on the alignment accuracy, optical loss, modal cross talk, background, photon budget, and the parameters that must be estimated simultaneously. The next chapter explains how these effects enter the experimental model and why the available aggregate first-order counts constrain only part of the underlying quantum description.
