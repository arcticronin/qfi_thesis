\chapter{Astrophysical Metrology: Exoplanet Detection}
\label{sec:astrophysical_metrology_spade}

We tested the quantum parameter estimation in astronomical observation, specifically the direct detection and characterization of exoplanets and close binary star systems~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. 
The fundamental limits are dictated not by detector sensitivity, but by the nature of light itself and the statistical properties of weak thermal sources~\cite{Tsang2016quantum}.
This chapter establishes the sub-Rayleigh imaging problem, models the optical field as a mixed quantum state, develops the star-centric SPADE receiver, and validates that receiver under experimentally relevant cross talk and count noise.
The data used in this chapter was given to our group by \textbf{ASI Matera (Italian Space Agency)}.
The challenge is exemplified by two primary observational fronts: high-contrast exoplanet detection and Space Domain Awareness (SDA).

\section{Problem Setting: High-Contrast Sources}

\subsection{The Exoplanet Characterization Problem}
Direct imaging of an exoplanet situated adjacent to its host star is exceptionally challenging due to the extreme disparity in their emission intensities. Let a primary bright source (the star, $A$) have an image-plane intensity $I_A$, and a secondary dim source (the exoplanet, $B$) have an intensity $I_B$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.
The physical system is characterized by the relative intensity ratio (or contrast ratio) $\epsilon$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon \coloneqq \frac{I_B}{I_A} \ll 1,
    \label{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon}
\end{equation}
representing a high dynamic range typically exceeding four to ten orders of magnitude ($\epsilon \sim 10^{-4}$ to $10^{-10}$)~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. 
When the angular separation between the star and the planet is small, their light fields overlap on the telescope's aperture, creating a composite field where the weak planetary signal is obscured by the classical shot noise of the overwhelming starlight~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. Detecting and characterizing the faint source therefore requires separating the two fields at sub-Rayleigh scales.

\subsection{Space Domain Awareness: Deep Sub-Rayleigh Constellations}
Beyond exoplanet detection, Space Domain Awareness (SDA) aims to continuously detect, track, catalog, and identify artificial space objects, for example satellites, spacecraft, and orbital debris, to ensure safe space operations~\cite{choi2024adaptive}. Ground-based imaging observations of these targets are severely restricted by their dim appearance, small separation, and dense distributions~\cite{choi2024adaptive}. These challenges render space targets as point-like objects in the sub-Rayleigh regime, possessing extreme brightness contrast but low photon budgets, often further degraded by atmospheric turbulence~\cite{choi2024adaptive}. To resolve these targets, the iterative Quantum-Accelerated Imaging (QAI) framework adaptively varies the measurement basis in response to initial data, maximizing the extracted Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) per photon~\cite{choi2024adaptive}.

\section{Rayleigh's Curse, Failure of Direct Imaging}
% ==============================================================================

According to the classical Rayleigh criterion, a diffraction-limited imaging system possesses a fundamental spatial resolution limit. When light from a point source passes through a telescope's circular pupil of diameter $R$, diffraction broadens the image into a finite spot on the image plane~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. Modeling this point-spread function (PSF) via a Gaussian approximation of the physical Airy disk, let the profile have a spatial width $w_0$. The Rayleigh limit is then reached when the transverse spatial separation $d$ between two point emitters becomes comparable to or smaller than $w_0$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/scripts_for_figures/rayleigh_resolved.png}\hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/scripts_for_figures/rayleigh_unresolved.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the Rayleigh criterion and the onset of Rayleigh's Curse in Direct Imaging (DI). The figure contrasts a resolved binary system (left, $d = 4.5$) with a sub-Rayleigh, unresolved system (right, $d = 2.5$) for a contrast ratio of $\epsilon = 0.4$. Top: 2D spatial intensity distribution on the image plane, representing the overlapping diffraction-limited point-spread functions (PSFs). Bottom: 1D cross-sectional intensity profiles along the axis of separation. The individual single-source PSFs are shown as dashed lines (blue-grey for the primary source $I_A$, grey for the secondary source $I_B$), while the solid green line represents the composite intensity measured during classical Direct Imaging. In the unresolved regime ($d \ll w_0$), the combined intensity profile merges into a single apparent peak. This visual blending demonstrates the fundamental loss of spatial distinguishability that drives the quadratic collapse of the classical Fisher Information ($\mathcal{I}_{\text{DI}}$) described in Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_fisher_collapse}.}
    \label{fig:rayleigh_criterion}
\end{figure}


In classical telescopy, detection is performed via \emph{Direct Imaging} (DI)—a pixel-by-pixel intensity measurement on the image plane using charge-coupled devices (CCDs)~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. Under DI, the single-photon probability density $p(x, y)$ on the detector array for a binary source separated along the $x$-axis is given by~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    p(x, y) = \eta \frac{e^{-y^2/(2w_0^2)}}{2\pi w_0^2} \left[ (1 - \epsilon) e^{-x^2/(2w_0^2)} + \epsilon e^{-(x-d)^2/(2w_0^2)} \right]
    \label{eq:di_probability_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $\eta \ll 1$ is the total single-photon detection probability.

The statistical consequence of this spatial overlap is known as \textbf{Rayleigh's Curse}~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. By evaluating the classical Fisher Information $\mathcal{I}_{\text{DI}}(d)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution}, we find that in the sub-Rayleigh regime ($d \ll w_0$), the Fisher information scales quadratically with the small parameter $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\text{DI}}(d) \approx \frac{\eta \epsilon^2}{w_0^2}.
    \label{eq:di_fisher_collapse}
\end{equation}
By the classical Cram\'{e}r-Rao bound, the minimum obtainable variance on the separation estimate scales as $\Delta d_{\text{DI}} \ge w_0 / (\epsilon \sqrt{\eta n})$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. For faint planets ($\epsilon \ll 1$), this statistical uncertainty diverges rapidly, rendering sub-Rayleigh characterization impossible under direct intensity measurements because the spatial intensity profile completely discards the phase information of the field.

\section{Representing Sensed Optical Fields as Mixed Quantum States}
% ==============================================================================

To link the spatial demultiplexing of an unbalanced binary source with our QFI formalism, we model the transverse optical field in the image plane of a ground-based astronomical telescope as a spatio-temporal mixed quantum state. We operate in the weak-field, faint-signal regime (such as that of direct exoplanet imaging under adaptive optics) where the probability of detecting more than one photon per temporal mode is negligibly small~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.

Following the single-photon vacuum mixture model, the optical state $\rho_d$ in a given spatio-temporal mode is represented by the non-normalized density operator:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_d = (1 - \eta)|0\rangle\langle 0| + \eta (1 - \epsilon) |\psi_0\rangle\langle \psi_0| + \eta \epsilon |\psi_d\rangle\langle \psi_d|,
    \label{eq:optical_density_matrix_chap}
\end{equation}
where $|0\rangle$ is the vacuum state, $|\psi_0\rangle$ is the spatial wave packet of a photon emitted by the primary source at the origin, and $|\psi_d\rangle$ is the packet of a photon emitted by the secondary source shifted by a transverse distance $d = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2}$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. Under a Gaussian point-spread function (PSF) of width $w_0$ (associated with the telescope's circular aperture), these spatial wavefunctions are expressed in the position basis as:
\begin{align}
    \langle x, y | \psi_0\rangle & = \psi_0(x, y) = \left( \frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2} \right)^{1/2} \exp\left( -\frac{x^2+y^2}{4w_0^2} \right), \label{eq:star_wavefunction}                     \\
    \langle x, y | \psi_d\rangle & = \psi_d(x, y) = \left( \frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2} \right)^{1/2} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - d_x)^2 + (y - d_y)^2}{4w_0^2} \right). \label{eq:planet_wavefunction}
\end{align}

In information geometry, the physical distinguishability of this parameterized state $\rho_d$ is quantified by the Bures metric:
\begin{equation}
    ds^2_{\text{Bures}}(\rho_d, \rho_{d+dd}) = \frac{1}{4} \mathcal{I}_{\text{exact}}(d) (dd)^2,
    \label{eq:bures_metric_optical}
\end{equation}
which is a quantum generalization of the classical Fisher information metric and is identical to the Fubini-Study metric when restricted to pure states. Computing the exact mixed-state QFI $\mathcal{I}_{\text{exact}}(d)$ classically requires solving the continuous Lyapunov equation for the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) $L_d$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    2 \partial_d \rho_d = \rho_d L_d + L_d \rho_d,
    \label{eq:lyapunov_opt_chap}
\end{equation}
which is notoriously difficult and scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ classically~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This classical bottleneck motivates both our NISQ variational bounds and our structured physical sorting measurements~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.

\section{SPADE Receiver and Star-Centric Alignment}
% ==============================================================================

The overwhelming challenge in sub-Rayleigh imaging is the high dynamic range: bright starlight forms a background that obscures the weak planetary signal~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. SPADE addresses this problem by resolving the field in a basis whose low-order modes separate the dominant symmetric component from displacement-sensitive components.

Specifically, we use a low-order Hermite--Gaussian (HG) modal cutoff consisting of the fundamental mode and the two first-order modes:
\begin{align}
    |HG_{00}\rangle & = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dxdy \, \psi_0(x, y) a^\dagger(x, y)|0\rangle, \label{eq:hg00_def}                                     \\
    |HG_{01}\rangle & = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dxdy \, \left(\frac{x - \delta_x}{w_0}\right) \psi_0(x,y) a^\dagger(x,y)|0\rangle, \label{eq:hg01_def} \\
    |HG_{10}\rangle & = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dxdy \, \left(\frac{y - \delta_y}{w_0}\right) \psi_0(x,y) a^\dagger(x,y)|0\rangle, \label{eq:hg10_def}
\end{align}
where $\delta_x, \delta_y$ account for residual spatial misalignment of the telescope's optical axis relative to the primary source~\cite{Santamaria2024single}, an intuitive visualization of them is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:hg_modes}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/scripts_for_figures/hg_modes_visualization.png}
    \caption{Visualization of the field amplitudes (top row) and spatial intensity distributions (bottom row) for the defined Hermite-Gaussian basis modes. To illustrate the physical effect of the residual spatial misalignment, the horizontal offset is set to $\delta_x = 0$, preserving the theoretical symmetry of the $|HG_{01}\rangle$ mode. In contrast, a non-zero vertical offset ($\delta_y = 0.3$) is applied to the $|HG_{10}\rangle$ mode, clearly demonstrating the resulting spatial shift of the mode profile along the $y$-axis.}
    \label{fig:hg_modes}
\end{figure}

These modes define two orthogonal projection operators acting on the incoming optical field~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{align}
    \Pi_0 & = |HG_{00}\rangle\langle HG_{00}| \quad \text{(The Dominant Star Subspace)}, \label{eq:pi0_def}                                         \\
    \Pi_1 & = |HG_{01}\rangle\langle HG_{01}| + |HG_{10}\rangle\langle HG_{10}| \quad \text{(The Perturbative Planet Subspace)}. \label{eq:pi1_def}
\end{align}


The fundamental port $\Pi_0$ records the dominant star contribution, while the first-order sector $\Pi_1$ carries the leading displacement-dependent signal. The relation of this physical modal cutoff to the spectral truncation used by VQFIE is developed separately in Chapter~\ref{ch:bridging_metrology_imaging}.

\subsection{Star-Centric Alignment}
Before measurement, the system is aligned with the bright star by tuning the spatial coordinates to maximize the single-photon count in the fundamental Hermite--Gaussian mode~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    p_0 = \langle HG_{00} | \rho_d | HG_{00} \rangle = \eta(1-\epsilon) e^{-\frac{\delta_x^2+\delta_y^2}{4w_0^2}} + \eta\epsilon e^{-\frac{(d_x-\delta_x)^2+(d_y-\delta_y)^2}{4w_0^2}}.
    \label{eq:p0_alignment}
\end{equation}

This alignment suppresses leakage from the dominant star component into the displacement-sensitive first-order sector. Chapter~\ref{ch:bridging_metrology_imaging} compares this optimization objective with the VQSE majorization objective, while keeping the optical modal cutoff distinct from a spectral-rank truncation.

\section{The 2-Basis HG SPADE Projection and Stochastic Error Propagation}
% ==============================================================================

Using the exact mixed-state QFI formulation, the metrological sensitivity about the separation $d$ is extracted via the SLD $L_d$ satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:lyapunov_opt_chap}~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The total exact QFI of the field, $\mathcal{I}_{\text{exact}}(d) = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho_d L_d^2)$, can be decomposed into classical and quantum components relative to the chosen measurement basis $\{\Pi_0, \Pi_1\}$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\text{exact}}(d) = \mathcal{I}_{\text{class}}(d) + \mathcal{I}_{\text{quant}}(d)
    \label{eq:qfi_decomposition_chap}
\end{equation}

The probability of detecting a photon in the first-order asymmetric modes (HG01, HG10) is given by:
\begin{equation}
    p_1 = \langle HG_{01} | \rho_d | HG_{01} \rangle + \langle HG_{10} | \rho_d | HG_{10} \rangle = $$
    $$ = \eta(1-\epsilon) \frac{\delta_x^2 + \delta_y^2}{4w_0^2} + \eta\epsilon \frac{(d_x - \delta_x)^2 + (d_y - \delta_y)^2}{4w_0^2}
    \label{eq:p1_asymmetric}
\end{equation}

Whereas the alignment procedure aims at reducing systematic error, we model the residual misalignment as a stochastic error~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. We assume that $\delta_x$ and $\delta_y$ are independent stochastic variables with zero mean and variance $\sigma_M^2/2$, where $\sigma_M$ quantifies the alignment precision~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[\delta_x] = \mathbb{E}[\delta_y] = 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[\delta_x^2] = \mathbb{E}[\delta_y^2] = \frac{\sigma_M^2}{2}
    \label{eq:stochastic_misalignment}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, physical demultiplexing is affected by mode cross talk $\chi$ (e.g., from Cailabs Proteus-C spatial demultiplexer, $\chi \approx 0.0035$), representing the probability that a photon in the fundamental mode HG00 is leaked and detected in either of the first-order modes~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This leaks a background term $\chi p_0$ into the first-order mode probability, which we incorporate by defining an effective spatial variance $\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 \coloneqq \sigma_M^2 + 4w_0^2 \chi.
    \label{eq:sigma_effective}
\end{equation}

Taking the expectation value over the stochastic misalignment and incorporating cross talk, the average detected fraction $f_1 = n_1/(\eta n)$ (where $n$ is the total photon budget) has a standard deviation of~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta f_1 = \frac{1}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{p_1 p_0}{\eta n}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 + \epsilon d^2}{4w_0^2 \eta n}}.
    \label{eq:std_dev_fraction}
\end{equation}

By error propagation, the uncertainty in the estimated separation $d$ (if $\epsilon$ is known) is~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta d = \left( \frac{\partial (p_1/\eta)}{\partial d} \right)^{-1} \Delta f_1 \approx \frac{2w_0^2}{\epsilon d} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 + \epsilon d^2}{4w_0^2 \eta n}}.
    \label{eq:propagation_uncertainty_d}
\end{equation}

Similarly, if $d$ is known, the uncertainty in the relative intensity estimate $\epsilon$ is~\cite{Santamaria2024single}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \epsilon = \left( \frac{\partial (p_1/\eta)}{\partial \epsilon} \right)^{-1} \Delta f_1 \approx \frac{4w_0^2}{d^2} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 + \epsilon d^2}{4w_0^2 \eta n}}.
    \label{eq:propagation_uncertainty_epsilon}
\end{equation}

Equations~\eqref{eq:propagation_uncertainty_d} and \eqref{eq:propagation_uncertainty_epsilon} prove that at sub-wavelength scales, the metrological precision of SPADE is ultimately limited by the hardware cross talk floor $\chi$, establishing an effective resolution limit of $d_{\text{min}} \approx w_0 \sqrt{\chi/\epsilon}$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.
