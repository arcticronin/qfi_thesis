\section{The exoplanet characterization problem}
Consider a star, denoted by $A$, with intensity $I_A$, and an exoplanet, denoted by $B$, with intensity $I_B$. This system is characterized by the relative intensity ratio (or contrast ratio) $\epsilon$
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon \coloneqq \frac{I_B}{I_A+I_B}
    \label{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon}
\end{equation}
Since the exoplanet is typically much fainter than its host star, so that $I_B \ll I_A$, $\epsilon$ can be approximated as ~\cite{Santamaria2024single}
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon \approx \frac{I_B}{I_A}
    \label{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon_approx}
\end{equation}
The approximation defined in Eq. \ref{eq:intensity_ratio_epsilon_approx} is the one used throughout the whole thesis.

\section{The sub-Rayleigh regime}
% ==============================================================================
When the angular separation between the star and the planet is small, their light fields overlap at the telescope's aperture, making the faint planet difficult to distinguish from the much brighter star. Detecting and characterizing the planet therefore requires resolving the two sources at sub-Rayleigh scales, i.e., separations smaller than the classic Rayleigh diffraction limit.
This limit arises because, due to diffraction at the telescope's finite aperture, light from a point source cannot be focused to an infinitesimal point. Instead, the telescope produces a finite intensity distribution. 
This distribution is the result of the incoming image and the telescope's own response, quantified by a function known as the point-spread function (PSF). The PSF is a property of the instrument, not of the source. For point-like sources, such as those considered here, the observed image is described directly by the PSF itself.
The PSF is commonly modeled as a Gaussian of width $w_0$ ~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The Rayleigh limit is then reached when the transverse separation $d$ between the two emitters becomes comparable to or smaller than $w_0$ ($d\lesssim w_0$)~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This transition from the resolved to the sub-Rayleigh regime is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:rayleigh_criterion}, where the increasing overlap between the two PSFs causes the distinct intensity peaks to merge into a single dominant profile.

\section{Why an intensity image is poorly conditioned}
Direct imaging is a common astronomical technique for detecting exoplanets. It involves capturing photographs or light spectra of an exoplanet by pointing a telescope directly at it.

Unfortunately, this detection method is not suitable for the sub-Rayleigh regime we want to treat in this thesis. To address this point more formally, below we compute the Fisher information for the system under study.

We consider the exoplanet and the star separated by a distance $d$ along the $x$ direction, with the star's position taken as known, and we model the PSF as a Gaussian of width $w_0$. Direct imaging records the transverse position at which each detected photon lands on the image plane. Conditional on a photon being detected, this position is distributed according to
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{DI}}(x,y\mid d,\epsilon)
    = \frac{e^{-y^2/(2w_0^2)}}{2\pi w_0^2}
    \left[(1-\epsilon)e^{-x^2/(2w_0^2)}
        +\epsilon e^{-(x-d)^2/(2w_0^2)}\right],
    \label{eq:di_probability_distribution}
\end{equation}
Note that Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution} describes only the \emph{spatial} distribution of a detected photon; it says nothing about whether a photon is detected at all in a given temporal mode. Here, a temporal mode can be thought of simply as one elementary time slot into which the incoming light is divided, short enough that at most one photon is expected in it; the probability that this slot actually yields a detected photon is denoted $\eta$, introduced below. In other words, direct imaging is fundamentally a position-basis measurement of the field: it reads out the intensity pattern pixel by pixel.

%This is in contrast to SPADE, discussed in the following sections, which instead projects the field onto a different, non-local set of spatial modes before the photon is counted.

Equation~\eqref{eq:di_probability_distribution} already makes the origin of the Rayleigh curse visually plausible: in the sub-Rayleigh regime $d\ll w_0$, the two Gaussian terms in brackets are nearly identical, so shifting the faint source by $d$ barely deforms the overall image; on top of this weak effect, the deformation itself enters only through the small prefactor $\epsilon$, since the companion contributes just a fraction $\epsilon$ of the detected photons. Both effects work in the same direction, and their combination is what ultimately suppresses the information about $d$ carried by the image.

This intuition is made quantitative by computing the Fisher information for the estimation of $d$, treating the star's position and the contrast $\epsilon$ as known. In the high-contrast, sub-Rayleigh limit, the Fisher information per temporal mode behaves as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{DI}}(d) \sim \frac{\eta\epsilon^2}{w_0^2},
    \qquad d\ll w_0,
    \label{eq:di_fisher_collapse}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is, as above, the probability that a given temporal mode yields a detected photon. The key feature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:di_fisher_collapse} is the quadratic dependence on $\epsilon$: the displacement signal is suppressed twice over, once because the companion is faint to begin with, and a second time because its already-weak imprint on the image must be estimated against the much brighter stellar background. This double suppression is the high-contrast manifestation of Rayleigh's curse, and it is precisely the limitation that motivates moving to a different measurement basis~\cite{Santamaria2024single,Tsang2016quantum}.

\section{Weak astronomical light as a spatial quantum state}

The same imaging problem can be described at the single-photon level. In the weak-light regime, a temporal mode is usually vacuum, and a non-vacuum event contains one photon from either incoherent source. Its density operator is
\begin{equation}
    \rho_d=(1-\eta)|0\rangle\langle0|
    +\eta(1-\epsilon)|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|
    +\eta\epsilon|\psi_d\rangle\langle\psi_d|
    \label{eq:optical_density_matrix_chap}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $|0\rangle$ is the vacuum,
    \item $|\psi_0\rangle$ is the spatial state from the star,
    \item $|\psi_d\rangle$ is the shifted spatial state from the planet.
\end{itemize}
The state is normalized. The mixture, rather than a coherent superposition of the two source states, expresses their mutual incoherence.

For the Gaussian PSF used for intuition in this chapter,
\begin{align}
    \langle x,y|\psi_0\rangle
     & =\left(\frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2}\right)^{1/2}
    \exp\!\left[-\frac{x^2+y^2}{4w_0^2}\right],
    \label{eq:star_wavefunction}                \\
    \langle x,y|\psi_d\rangle
     & =\left(\frac{1}{2\pi w_0^2}\right)^{1/2}
    \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-d_x)^2+(y-d_y)^2}{4w_0^2}\right].
    \label{eq:planet_wavefunction}
\end{align}
This description does not assume that an astronomical field is prepared in a laboratory quantum state. It is a compact statistical description of the spatial degree of freedom carried by each detected photon. It makes explicit that the choice of measurement basis is part of the estimation problem.

\section{SPADE: measuring in spatial modes}

Spatial-mode demultiplexing (SPADE) replaces pixel measurements with projections onto a set of orthogonal transverse modes~\cite{Tsang2016quantum,Santamaria2024single}. The key design choice is to use modes matched to the PSF and to centre the lowest-order mode on the bright source. For a Gaussian PSF, the natural family is the Hermite--Gaussian (HG) basis.

\subsection{The Hermite--Gaussian spatial basis}

\begin{definition}[Hermite--Gaussian spatial basis]
    The transverse spatial state of one detected photon is described by a square-integrable complex wavefunction of the image-plane coordinates $(x,y)$.  The Hermite--Gaussian modes form a complete orthonormal basis of this infinite-dimensional spatial Hilbert space:
    \begin{equation}
        \langle HG_{mn}|HG_{pq}\rangle=\delta_{mp}\delta_{nq},
        \qquad
        |\psi\rangle=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}c_{mn}|HG_{mn}\rangle,
        \qquad
        c_{mn}=\langle HG_{mn}|\psi\rangle
        \label{eq:hg_complete_basis}
    \end{equation}
    Here $m$ and $n$ are non-negative integers that label the modal order in the $x$ and $y$ directions.  The functions are a Gaussian envelope multiplied by Hermite polynomials.  Increasing either index introduces additional transverse structure, it does not introduce an additional photon.
\end{definition}

The fundamental mode $HG_{00}$ has the same Gaussian shape as an aligned point-source PSF.  The first-order modes have one sign change in their \emph{field amplitude} along one axis.  Their intensity is non-negative, the sign change matters because a mode sorter interferometrically projects the incoming field onto these amplitudes before the final photon counts are recorded.

For a small displacement of a Gaussian PSF, the modal expansion makes the SPADE mechanism explicit:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi_{\boldsymbol d}\rangle
    \simeq |HG_{00}\rangle
    +\frac{d_x}{2w_0}|HG_{10}\rangle
    +\frac{d_y}{2w_0}|HG_{01}\rangle
    +\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{w_0^2}\right),
    \qquad d\ll w_0.
    \label{eq:hg_small_displacement_expansion}
\end{equation}
Thus, a small displacement transfers field amplitude out of the star-centred fundamental mode and into the first-order modes.  Contributions from higher total HG orders enter at progressively higher powers of $d/w_0$.  This is why low-order modes are physically informative in the sub-Rayleigh regime, while the full HG basis remains infinite.

The fundamental mode is the star-centred PSF, while the two first-order modes are its displacement-sensitive directions:
\begin{align}
    \langle x,y|HG_{00}\rangle
     & =\psi_0(x,y),
    \label{eq:hg00_def}           \\
    \langle x,y|HG_{01}\rangle
     & =\frac{y}{w_0}\psi_0(x,y),
    \label{eq:hg01_def}           \\
    \langle x,y|HG_{10}\rangle
     & =\frac{x}{w_0}\psi_0(x,y).
    \label{eq:hg10_def}
\end{align}
These position-space wavefunctions are equivalent to defining one-photon creation operators, but avoid introducing second-quantized notation that is not needed for the present measurement model.  A practical mode sorter implements the HG projections optically and directs the resulting modes to separate photon-counting ports.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/scripts_for_figures/hg_modes_visualization.png}
    \caption{The fundamental and first-order Hermite--Gaussian spatial modes. The first-order modes have a sign change across one transverse axis, so a small displacement of a PSF away from the fundamental-mode centre transfers amplitude into them.}
    \label{fig:hg_modes}
\end{figure}

It is useful to group the lowest-order ports as
\begin{align}
    \Pi_0 & =|HG_{00}\rangle\langle HG_{00}|,
    \label{eq:pi0_def}                        \\
    \Pi_1 & =|HG_{01}\rangle\langle HG_{01}|
    +|HG_{10}\rangle\langle HG_{10}|.
    \label{eq:pi1_def}
\end{align}
The low-order modal cutoff is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{\leq1}\coloneqq\Pi_0+\Pi_1,
    \qquad
    \Pi_{\mathrm{rest}}\coloneqq\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\leq1}.
    \label{eq:hg_low_order_cutoff}
\end{equation}
It retains three modes---one fundamental and two first-order modes---while $\Pi_{\mathrm{rest}}$ contains every higher HG order.  This is a \emph{fixed physical measurement sector}, not a spectral-rank truncation of $\rho_d$.  The distinction is essential in the practical QFI analysis of the next chapter.

When the sorter is aligned to the star, the star predominantly occupies $HG_{00}$. A small planetary displacement populates the first-order sector.  The probability for a temporal mode to produce a detection in that sector is
\begin{equation}
    p_1\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_d\Pi_1]
    \simeq \eta\epsilon\frac{d_x^2+d_y^2}{4w_0^2},
    \qquad d\ll w_0.
    \label{eq:p1_asymmetric}
\end{equation}
Thus the first-order counts provide a direct displacement-sensitive observable without requiring the planet to form a visually separated intensity peak.  In the leading approximation, the $HG_{10}$ and $HG_{01}$ ports carry the $d_x^2$ and $d_y^2$ contributions separately, whereas their sum depends on $d_x^2+d_y^2$.  Photon counts in these ports alone are insensitive to the sign of a displacement along either axis; resolving that ambiguity requires a measurement sensitive to the relevant intermodal coherence, together with a defined spatial reference.  Higher-order modes, background rejection, and different modal bases can also be useful; the low-order picture is the simplest illustration of the SPADE principle.

\subsection{Alignment and ordinary SPADE inference}

The required reference frame is normally obtained by centring the fundamental mode on the bright source.  Let $\bmdelta=(\delta_x,\delta_y)$ denote the displacement of the mode-sorter axis relative to the stellar centroid.  The fundamental-port probability is then
\begin{equation}
    p_0\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_d\Pi_0]
    =\eta(1-\epsilon)e^{-(\delta_x^2+\delta_y^2)/(4w_0^2)}
    +\eta\epsilon e^{-[(d_x-\delta_x)^2+(d_y-\delta_y)^2]/(4w_0^2)}.
    \label{eq:p0_alignment}
\end{equation}
Maximising this count is a natural star-centric alignment rule. It reduces leakage of the bright star into the displacement-sensitive ports, but it does not by itself estimate the planet parameters.

After alignment, a standard SPADE experiment records the counts in ports such as $HG_{00}$, $HG_{10}$, and $HG_{01}$. These counts, or their relative frequencies, are compared with a calibrated forward model for the modal probabilities. A likelihood fit then estimates $\boldsymbol d$ and/or $\epsilon$; uncertainties arise from finite photon number as well as imperfect alignment, loss, background, and mode cross talk. The details of how those effects are modelled and how QFI is used to assess the resulting procedure belong to the following application chapter, rather than to this physical introduction.

\begin{remark}[Scope of the SPADE advantage]
    SPADE does not remove diffraction, create photons, or make every practical receiver optimal. Its advantage is to access a spatial-mode statistic that is poorly represented in a raw intensity image. Whether that advantage survives in a concrete instrument depends on its alignment, loss, cross talk, photon budget, and the parameters treated as unknown.
\end{remark}

\section{Takeaway}

High-contrast sub-Rayleigh imaging is an estimation problem in which the informative change of a faint source is hidden inside the much larger intensity profile of a bright source. Direct imaging treats the field position by position and becomes poorly conditioned in this regime. SPADE instead sorts the same incoming photons into a PSF-matched spatial basis, where low-order mode populations reveal small displacements. This establishes the physical baseline for the QFI-based method and its experimental implementation developed in the next chapter.
