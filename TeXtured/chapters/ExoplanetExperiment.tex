\chapter{Practical SPADE: From an Initial TQFI Idea to a Channel Model}
\label{ch:exoplanet_experiment}

Chapter~\ref{ch:astrophysical_theory} introduced high-contrast sub-Rayleigh imaging and the ideal SPADE measurement.  The present chapter describes what happened when that theory was confronted with the available experimental record.  The initial plan was to use a few measured Hermite--Gaussian (HG) modes to build a reduced optical description and connect it with the truncated-QFI methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}.  The data, however, contain only one aggregate count channel.  They are not a measurement of a two-mode quantum state.

This limitation does not make the data useless, but it changes the question.  Rather than reconstructing a state that was not measured, we model the complete path from the optical source to the stored count.  The repeated counts can then test whether a specified channel configuration is compatible with the experiment.  QFI is calculated only for the quantum state associated with such a model and is used as a theoretical limit and a measurement-design diagnostic.

\begin{infobox}{The reasoning followed in this chapter}
    The analysis proceeds in four stages:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item state the original low-mode/TQFI objective;
        \item identify exactly what the count file contains and why it is insufficient for that objective;
        \item replace the attempted state reconstruction with a sequence of physical, measurement, and classical channels;
        \item test complete channel configurations against the repetitions and then compare their model QFI with the CFI of different SPADE measurements.
    \end{enumerate}
    This order is important: the QFI calculation is the final model-based step, not a quantity extracted directly from the count variance.
\end{infobox}

\begin{remark}[What this chapter establishes]
    The recorded first-order SPADE counts require a nonzero baseline and are overdispersed relative to ideal Poisson counting.  A sequential channel configuration with an explicit overdispersion factor is compatible with the repeated data when its independent calibration assumptions are fixed.  For the corresponding truncated optical model, complete ideal SPADE is nearly QFI-optimal, whereas the recorded binary first-order channel can retain much less information because of leakage and count noise.  These are conditional model-validation and measurement-design results.  They are not a direct experimental measurement of QFI, a unique estimate of every channel parameter, or a data-derived TQFI calculation.
\end{remark}

\section{What We Wanted to Measure and What Was Recorded}
\label{sec:spade_question_record}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection{The original low-mode idea}

The initial objective was motivated by the low-order SPADE receiver of Chapter~\ref{ch:astrophysical_theory} and the rank-truncation methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}.  A displaced point-spread function occupies an infinite Hermite--Gaussian (HG) ladder, but low orders carry the leading displacement dependence near the aligned sub-Rayleigh regime.  It was therefore natural to ask whether the experimentally accessible low-order ports could be used to construct a reduced state and evaluate a truncated-QFI-type quantity.

\subsection{The available count record}

The available ASI record is more limited than that objective requires.  It is a cube of repeated aggregate first-order counts,
\begin{equation}
    n_1=n_{HG_{01}}+n_{HG_{10}},
    \qquad
    \texttt{H\_data}[d_a,\epsilon,r]=n_1,
    \label{eq:spade_recorded_count}
\end{equation}
with $21\times25\times100=52{,}500$ measurements.  The dimensionless signed scan coordinate ranges over $d_a\in[-\frac{2}{3},\frac{2}{3}]$, the faint-source fraction over $\epsilon\in[0.00427,0.09593]$, and $r$ indexes the repetitions.  The scan convention and PSF scale are retained as calibrated analysis coordinates. The model below makes any conversion to a physical width explicit.

\noindent The structure of the record can be summarized before any modelling:
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 programmed setting (d_a, epsilon)
                 |
                 v
          [SPADE apparatus]
            |    |    |
            |    |    +--> higher modes / no click  (not stored)
            |    +-------> HG00                     (not stored)
            +-----------> HG01 + HG10               (stored only as n1)

 The same setting is repeated 100 times.
\end{Verbatim}

The missing information is not merely inconvenient.  A nonzero observed $n_1$ at $d_a=0$ can arise from dark counts, bright-mode leakage, higher-order leakage, incident flux, or imperfect alignment. \subsection{What an accessible-mode experiment would require}

For a fixed accessible modal set $\mathcal{M}$, a physically meaningful reduction of the optical state would be
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{acc}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}|HG_j\rangle\langle HG_j|,
    \qquad
    \rho_{\mathrm{acc}}(d_a,\epsilon)
    =P_{\mathrm{acc}}\rho(d_a,\epsilon)P_{\mathrm{acc}}.
    \label{eq:spade_accessible_projection}
\end{equation}
For example, $\mathcal{M}=\{HG_{00},HG_{01},HG_{10}\}$ would retain a reference mode and the two-dimensional first-order sector.  Crucially, the complete measurement must also retain the complementary outcome $\mathbb{I}-P_{\mathrm{acc}}$.  Otherwise, loss and population in unmeasured higher modes are silently postselected away, and potentially relevant separation information is discarded.

This would be an interesting experiment: calibrated counts in every selected mode and in the complement would define the classical Fisher information (CFI) of the actual multi-outcome SPADE POVM.  With an independently justified source model or coherence-sensitive data, it could also support a study of the reduced quantum state.

\noindent In diagrammatic form, the required measurement would be
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
                         +--> HG00
                         +--> HG01
 rho(d_a, epsilon) -->   +--> HG10       --> calibrated outcome
      SPADE POVM         +--> ...             probabilities --> CFI
                         +--> complement

 The complement is an outcome of the experiment, not discarded data.
\end{Verbatim}

\begin{remark}[Accessible modal projection is not rank-$m$ TQFI]
    The TQFI construction in Section~\ref{sec:bounds} retains the $m$ dominant \emph{eigenvectors} of $\rho(\theta)$ and compares two parameterized states on that fixed state eigenspace.  A finite HG set instead retains a fixed, experimentally chosen \emph{measurement basis}.  HG modes need not be eigenvectors of the mixed source state, and the two first-order ports are not automatically its two principal components.  A numerical HG cutoff is likewise different again: it is a controlled representation of the infinite spatial Hilbert space, not a rank-$m$ TQFI projection.
\end{remark}

The present file cannot construct $\rho_{\mathrm{acc}}$ from data.  In particular, dividing $n_1$ by an assumed photon number and then setting $p_0=1-p_1$ would create two artificial probabilities.  The quantity called $p_0$ would silently combine vacuum, missed photons, $HG_{00}$ population, higher modes, and background events.  Even a correctly normalized set of modal probabilities would give only the diagonal of the state in the HG measurement basis; it would not supply the coherences needed for a general density-matrix QFI calculation.

\subsection{How the question was reformulated}

The limitation changes the question rather than ending the analysis.  We use the available repetitions to test models for the final recorded count.  For each explicit model configuration, we can then propagate the corresponding source state through named quantum channels, calculate its QFI, and compare this model limit with the CFI of ideal and imperfect SPADE measurements.  Thus the result is a calibrated-model study, not a claim of state reconstruction.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 original question:
 measured low modes --> reconstructed reduced state --> TQFI
                              X
                    not supported by n1 alone

 revised question:
 repeated n1 counts --> test a complete channel model
                                   |
                                   v
                         model state and QFI
                                   |
                                   v
                   compare ideal and practical SPADE CFI
\end{Verbatim}

\section{From Optical Source to Recorded Count}
\label{sec:spade_sequential_channels}
% ==============================================================================

The revised analysis follows the complete experiment in its physical order.  It separates four different operations: encoding the source parameters in an optical state, changing that state through quantum channels, measuring it with SPADE, and finally recording a noisy classical count.  Calling all four operations \emph{noise} would hide which object is being changed and which information quantity can be assigned to it.

\noindent The complete process used in the analysis is
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 theta = (d_a, epsilon)
          |
          v
 [Source preparation]
          |
          | quantum state rho_S
          v
 [Photon loss] --> [Sorter alignment] --> [HG dephasing]
          |                                      |
          | quantum state rho_q                  | optional environments
          v                                      v
 [Imperfect SPADE POVM] --> outcome Y --> [Background + count noise]
                                                   |
                                                   v
                                      recorded aggregate count n1
\end{Verbatim}

For parameter-independent loss, alignment, and dephasing settings, the corresponding information hierarchy is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_S]
    \geq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{loss}}]
    = \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{align}}]
    \geq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{q}}]
    \geq \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{C}}(Y).
    \label{eq:spade_information_processing_chain}
\end{equation}
The equality follows because a fixed unitary alignment changes the basis but not the QFI.  The last term is the CFI of the idealized measurement outcome per launched photon.  The final count CFI is instead a per-acquisition quantity after $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ trials and classical count noise, so it can be compared with $N_{\mathrm{in}}\mathcal{F}_Q$ only after the photon-number and detector assumptions have been stated.

\subsection{Source sectors and a controlled numerical basis}

The spatial field is not intrinsically a two-mode system.  In principle, it occupies the infinite basis
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{spatial}}
    =\operatorname{span}\{
    |HG_{00}\rangle,|HG_{01}\rangle,|HG_{10}\rangle,
    |HG_{02}\rangle,\ldots\}.
    \label{eq:spade_infinite_hg_space}
\end{equation}
The physical source is represented in this HG basis with one displacement direction aligned to the experimental scan.  For numerical work, modes are retained through HG order $12$, together with a vacuum state and an unresolved higher-order tail.  The representative QFI calculations are stable when the cutoff is increased from order $8$ to order $12$.  This is a computational approximation to the infinite space; it is not the rank-$m$ TQFI truncation discussed above.  The tail preserves trace and does not imply that all higher HG modes form one physical mode.

Because the record stores $n_{HG_{01}}+n_{HG_{10}}$ rather than its two components, the count-facing calculation uses a one-axis effective description of the aggregate first-order sector.  It does not reconstruct a two-dimensional spatial density matrix.

Before loss, the model source has the form
\begin{equation}
    \rho_S(d_a,\epsilon)
    =\left(1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)|HG_{00}\rangle\langle HG_{00}|
    +\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}|u_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}\rangle\langle u_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}|,
    \label{eq:spade_source_state}
\end{equation}
where $|u_{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}\rangle$ is the displaced Gaussian PSF.  The source components are mutually incoherent, as in the optical model of Chapter~\ref{ch:astrophysical_theory}.

For the data-facing sector model, the effective coordinates are
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{d_a-d_0}{w_{\mathrm{scale}}},
    \qquad
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=\epsilon^{\alpha},
    \qquad
    \mu=\frac{d_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}{16},
    \label{eq:spade_effective_coordinates}
\end{equation}
where $d_0$, $w_{\mathrm{scale}}$, and $\alpha$ represent a displacement offset, a PSF-width rescaling, and a source-ratio calibration exponent.  The factor defining $\mu$ follows the PSF convention of the analysis code; $w_{\mathrm{scale}}$ carries the conversion from the scan coordinate to that convention.  The grouped source probabilities are
\begin{align}
    p_0               & =1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}e^{-\mu}, \\
    p_1               & =\epsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}\mu e^{-\mu},                       \\
    p_{\mathrm{tail}} & =1-p_0-p_1.
    \label{eq:spade_sector_probabilities}
\end{align}
Here $p_1$ denotes the total first-order sector used by the stored aggregate count.  This simple sector description is used for likelihood intuition; the QFI calculation uses the converged multi-mode state rather than treating Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_sector_probabilities} as a two-mode quantum state.

The three probabilities have a direct interpretation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $p_0$ is the bright/reference sector, dominated by $HG_{00}$;
    \item $p_1$ is the leading separation-sensitive first-order sector;
    \item $p_{\mathrm{tail}}$ groups the unresolved higher orders for the count model.
\end{itemize}
At perfect alignment, $d_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$ and therefore $\mu=0$, so the ideal model gives $p_1=0$.  The observed nonzero count at this point cannot be explained as an ideal first-order signal.  This simple observation is what forces a baseline mechanism---background, leakage, or both---into the practical model.

\subsection{The model, one step at a time}

\paragraph{1. Photon loss.}
The prepared source contains one photon in the spatial HG system.  A photon survives with probability $\eta$ and otherwise enters a vacuum state:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\mathrm{loss}}
    =\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{loss},\eta}[\rho_S]
    =\eta\rho_S+(1-\eta)|\mathrm{vac}\rangle\langle\mathrm{vac}|.
    \label{eq:spade_loss_channel}
\end{equation}
This is a completely positive trace-preserving erasure channel.  It is the correct position for $\eta$ when $\eta$ denotes the survival of a launched photon.

\paragraph{2. Static sorter misalignment.}
A fixed displacement between the optical field and the mode sorter is represented by
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\mathrm{align}}
    =U_{\mathrm{align}}(\delta_{\mathrm{sorter}})
    \rho_{\mathrm{loss}}
    U_{\mathrm{align}}^{\dagger}(\delta_{\mathrm{sorter}}).
    \label{eq:spade_alignment_channel}
\end{equation}
This map is unitary and parameter independent.  It therefore preserves state QFI, although it can make a fixed SPADE measurement less informative.  The sorter offset $\delta_{\mathrm{sorter}}$ is conceptually different from the source-coordinate offset $d_0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_effective_coordinates}.  The aggregate record tends to correlate them, so they cannot both be interpreted physically without an independent alignment reference.

\paragraph{3. HG-basis dephasing.}
The visibility parameter $v$ controls loss of coherence in the HG basis:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\mathrm{q}}
    =\mathcal{E}_{\phi,v}[\rho_{\mathrm{align}}]
    =v\rho_{\mathrm{align}}
    +(1-v)\operatorname{diag}_{\mathrm{HG}}(\rho_{\mathrm{align}}).
    \label{eq:spade_quantum_sequence}
\end{equation}
This channel preserves HG populations but suppresses off-diagonal terms.  It can change QFI, which depends on the complete state, but it cannot be identified from this diagonal aggregate count alone.

\paragraph{4. Imperfect SPADE measurement.}
The mode sorter does not report the optical state directly.  It produces a classical outcome.  Let
$\Pi_{HG_1}\coloneqq\Pi_{HG_{01}}+\Pi_{HG_{10}}$.  The reported first-order event is modelled by
\begin{equation}
    M_1=l_0\Pi_{HG_{00}}+l_1\Pi_{HG_1}
    +l_{\mathrm{tail}}\Pi_{\mathrm{higher}},
    \qquad
    M_{\mathrm{other}}=\mathbb{I}-M_1,
    \label{eq:spade_imperfect_povm}
\end{equation}
where $0\leq l_0,l_1,l_{\mathrm{tail}}\leq1$.  The coefficient $l_0$ is leakage of the bright $HG_{00}$ sector into the reported first-order channel, $l_1$ is the desired first-order transmission, and $l_{\mathrm{tail}}$ describes higher-order events reported in that same channel.  These coefficients belong to the measurement POVM.  They are not automatically quantum noise acting on the state.

\paragraph{5. Classical background and count fluctuations.}
After the POVM, the model has a classical event probability.  The predicted count mean and working variance are
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_1=b+N_{\mathrm{in}}\operatorname{Tr}[M_1\rho_{\mathrm{q}}],
    \qquad
    \operatorname{Var}(n_1)=F\lambda_1,
    \label{eq:spade_count_channel}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is a background count, $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the number of launched photons per acquisition, and $F$ is the Fano factor.  A normal likelihood with this mean and variance is used as a quasi-Gaussian approximation for the present repeated-count regime.  This is a statistical model for the observed fluctuations, not a claim that photon counting is fundamentally Gaussian.

For identity alignment and dephasing, Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_count_channel} reduces to the transparent sector expression
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_1=b+N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta
    \left(l_0p_0+l_1p_1+l_{\mathrm{tail}}p_{\mathrm{tail}}\right).
    \label{eq:spade_sector_count_model}
\end{equation}
This expression also makes the parameter degeneracy visible.  The observed scale depends on products such as $N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta l_1$, while the baseline can contain both $b$ and $N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta l_0$.  The current count channel cannot separate all of these factors.  For data exploration, it is therefore useful to write
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_1=B+A_1p_1+A_{\mathrm{tail}}p_{\mathrm{tail}},
    \label{eq:spade_effective_count_model}
\end{equation}
where, approximately at small displacement,
\begin{equation}
    B\simeq b+N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta l_0,\qquad
    A_1=N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta(l_1-l_0),\qquad
    A_{\mathrm{tail}}=N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta(l_{\mathrm{tail}}-l_0).
    \label{eq:spade_effective_parameter_combinations}
\end{equation}
These are identifiable combinations and useful diagnostic parameters.  They are not unique measurements of dark counts, leakage, or efficiency.

\begin{remark}[Where information can be lost]
    A parameter-independent unitary alignment map leaves the QFI of the quantum state invariant, but it can reduce the CFI of the fixed downstream SPADE POVM.  The loss and dephasing maps can reduce QFI.  In contrast, the imperfect POVM and the classical count channel do not change the pre-measurement state QFI; they reduce the information available in the observed data.  These distinctions are why all imperfections should not be represented by one generic ``noise'' box.
\end{remark}

\section{What the Repeated Counts Taught Us}
\label{sec:spade_data_diagnostics}
% ==============================================================================

The final model was not chosen at the beginning.  It was built by asking what each simpler description failed to explain:
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 ideal HG1 response
        |
        +--> fails at d_a = 0: a baseline is missing
        v

        |
        +--> describes most of the mean, but not all response variation
        v
 test offset, epsilon calibration, and higher-order tail
        |
        +--> bound-hitting and negative tail expose missing calibrations
        v
 inspect all repetitions
        |
        +--> variance exceeds the mean: Poisson noise is inadequate
        v
 constrained sequential channel + overdispersed count model
\end{Verbatim}

The raw data already rule out two important idealizations.  Across the scan, the mean recorded first-order count ranges from $6.93$ to $19.44$, while the median count at zero displacement is $8.17$. Since ideal Poisson counts would have $F=1$, their fluctuations are too small to describe the repetitions. \subsection{What the successive fits showed}

The first check was deliberately phenomenological.  At fixed $\epsilon$, the quadratic curve $n_1=a+b d_a^2$ can be rewritten as $n_1=\chi\eta_n+c\epsilon d_a^2$ when both coefficients are free.  The two expressions contain the same fitting freedom; the second form only gives the coefficients a possible physical interpretation.  For the reference $\eta_n=40{,}000$, the pooled fit gives a baseline $\chi\eta_n=8.297$ counts, $\chi=2.074\times10^{-4}$, response coefficient $c=272.83$, and $R^2=0.8850$.  The baseline-like coefficient varies by only $4.4\%$ across curves, whereas the response coefficient varies by $52.6\%$.  Thus the baseline is comparatively stable, but a single response coefficient does not describe all source ratios.  This is evidence that the simple response model is incomplete; it is not an independent measurement of $\chi$.

\noindent\emph{Plot brief.} Insert the paper-style quadratic separation calibrations and linear intensity calibrations.  Show the approximately stable offsets and the visibly less stable response slopes, rather than presenting the regressions as a complete physical calibration.

The model sequence makes the same point quantitatively.  A scaled ideal HG1 response has $R^2=-7.1845$ because it lacks a baseline and is worse than predicting one constant mean.  Adding a baseline and first-order contrast raises the fit to $R^2=0.8858$.  Allowing a source-ratio exponent and a small signed-displacement offset raises it only to $R^2=0.8904$, with effective baseline $8.282$, HG1 scale $7716$, offset $0.0073$, and exponent $1.201$.  These numbers summarize the observed mean surface.  They should not be read as unique measurements of separate optical components.

Attempts to fit the higher-order tail illustrate why physical constraints matter.  A flexible tail term raises a mean-model fit from $R^2=0.8858$ to $0.9127$, but requires $A_{\mathrm{tail}}=-221290$.  A negative effective leakage is not a physical channel parameter; it is a warning that the flexible term is absorbing missing structure.  Likewise, exploratory fits that let the PSF scale float can drive it to a bound.  Such behaviour is diagnostic evidence for missing calibration information, not evidence for an exotic optical effect.

\begin{remark}[What the effective fits are for]
    The effective models answer a limited but useful question: which features must a physical model explain?  Here the answer is a baseline, non-Poisson repeated-count noise, and response variations that cannot safely be attributed to one uncalibrated component.  They are therefore used to guide constrained channel models, not to replace them.
\end{remark}

\section{Testing a Complete Channel Configuration}
\label{sec:spade_conditional_inference}
% ==============================================================================

The aggregate count does not determine every parameter in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_sector_count_model}.  The practical solution is therefore conditional inference: fix the quantities supplied by independent calibration or by an explicitly declared reference scenario, fit only the small number of remaining identifiable parameters, and test the complete configuration against all $52{,}500$ observations.

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 independent calibrations or stated assumptions
                       |
                       v
          fix N_in, b, l0, l1, ltail,
             geometry, F, and visibility
                       |
 repeated n1 data --> fit eta --> profile interval
                       |
                       v
       simulate the fitted complete configuration
                       |
                       v
 compare simulations with data --> bootstrap p-value
                       |
                       v
      if compatible: calculate model QFI and SPADE CFI
\end{Verbatim}

This procedure prevents degenerate products such as $N_{\mathrm{in}}\eta l_1$ from being reported as separately measured quantities.  It also changes the meaning of the result: the analysis tests a complete assumed configuration, not one parameter in isolation.

\subsection{A reference configuration}

To demonstrate the procedure, one transparent reference scenario fixes
\[
    N_{\mathrm{in}}=40{,}000,\quad b=0,\quad
    l_0=l_{\mathrm{tail}}=0.002,\quad l_1=1,
\]
together with $d_0=0$, $w_{\mathrm{scale}}=1$, $\alpha=1$, $F=2.6$, and $v=1$.  These values define the scenario; the current count file does not measure all of them independently.  Once they are fixed, the remaining survival parameter has the conditional maximum-likelihood estimate
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\eta}=0.10480,
    \qquad
    \eta\in[0.10408,0.10553]
    \quad\text{at }99.9\%\text{ profile confidence}.
    \label{eq:spade_conditional_eta}
\end{equation}
The fitted configuration has Pearson statistic $52{,}171.9$, compared with bootstrap median $52{,}440.2$, and a parametric-bootstrap $p$-value of $0.8060$ in $200$ simulations.  In plain terms, simulated datasets from this fitted scenario commonly look at least as discrepant as the real dataset.  The configuration is therefore compatible with the repeated counts.

For the identical optical assumptions but ideal Poisson fluctuations ($F=1$), the conditional estimate changes to $\hat{\eta}=0.11258$ and the bootstrap gives $p=0.00498$.  This rejects the ideal-Poisson count-noise assumption for this configuration.  It does \emph{not} by itself select a value of $\eta$, because changing the fixed input flux, leakage, background, PSF, or source-ratio calibration changes the conditional estimate.

\begin{remark}[Meaning of the bootstrap result]
    A large bootstrap $p$-value means that the configuration has not been rejected by this discrepancy test.  It does not prove that the configuration is unique or physically true.  Likewise, the narrow interval in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_conditional_eta} measures statistical precision only after all the other values have been fixed.  It does not contain their systematic calibration uncertainty.
\end{remark}

The channel implementation also provides useful conditional profiles.  Varying the HG dephasing visibility over $v=0,0.5,1$ leaves the aggregate-count likelihood flat and returns the same $\hat\eta=0.10480$, as expected because this measurement records only diagonal modal information.  Its model QFI changes only from $0.00245187$ to $0.00245203$ at the reference setting.  In contrast, a profile of fixed sorter displacement, while holding the source offset fixed, has its optimum close to zero; the change in negative log likelihood is $15.1$ at $-0.02$ and $66.6$ at $+0.02$.  Releasing both offsets would reintroduce their degeneracy, which is why an alignment reference is needed.

\section{What QFI Adds After the Model Test}
\label{sec:spade_qfi_role}

The count likelihood and the QFI answer different questions.  The likelihood asks whether a complete model could plausibly have produced the recorded repetitions.  The QFI asks how much separation information is present in the quantum state predicted by that model before it is measured.  For the post-quantum-channel state in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spade_quantum_sequence}, the SLD formalism of Chapter~\ref{ch:MathBackground} gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Q}}(d_a)
    =\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Q}}\!\left[\rho_{\mathrm{q}}(d_a,\epsilon)\right].
    \label{eq:spade_model_qfi}
\end{equation}
It is a per-launched-photon upper bound for the assumed model.  It is not obtained by substituting the empirical count variance into a QFI formula.

\noindent For every configuration that remains compatible with the counts, the comparison has the following structure:
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 assumed post-channel state rho_q(d_a, epsilon)
                       |
                       +--> model QFI: maximum allowed information
                       |
                       +--> full ideal-SPADE CFI
                       |
                       +--> ideal first-order CFI
                       |
                       +--> imperfect binary-readout CFI
                       |
                       +--> final noisy-count CFI
\end{Verbatim}
The first quantity belongs to the quantum state.  The remaining quantities belong to increasingly practical measurements or count models.  Their comparison locates where information is lost; it does not turn the final count CFI into QFI.

\begin{infobox}{What QFI does and does not mean in this chapter}
    The count record tests the final likelihood of an assumed channel configuration.  It does not tomographically reconstruct the quantum wire before the SPADE POVM, and it cannot yield an ``experimental QFI'' from $n_1$ alone.  Once a configuration passes the count test, its model QFI can be compared with the CFI of (i) a complete ideal HG measurement, (ii) an ideal first-order measurement, (iii) the imperfect binary POVM, and (iv) the final noisy count distribution.  This identifies where information is lost and which additional measurements would be valuable.
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Numerical and channel checks}

The HG order-$12$ QFI calculation is checked at twelve regular representative points by an independent finite-fidelity calculation.  The largest relative SLD--fidelity discrepancy is $0.126\%$.  The loss channel is trace preserving to $2.22\times10^{-16}$, the smallest propagated density-matrix eigenvalue is $-5.12\times10^{-27}$ (numerical zero), and the quantum implementation reproduces the sector model exactly when alignment and dephasing are identities.  These checks validate the implementation of the stated model; they are not an additional experimental measurement.

At exactly $d_a=0$, the source model changes rank.  The derivative SLD QFI and a finite-fidelity quotient are then non-regular limits, so this point is reported separately rather than treated as a numerical failure.  Information-ratio plots should focus on nonzero separation or explicitly mark this boundary point.

The ideal first-order fraction and the imperfect binary-readout fraction answer different questions.  The former asks how much information a perfect first-order projection would retain.  The latter asks how much survives after the assumed $HG_{00}$ leakage and binary aggregation.  Since the planetary signal is small compared with the leakage-induced baseline, the practical fraction can be far lower.  This difference is a measurement-design result, not a contradiction.

\section{What Was Learned and What Should Be Measured Next}
\label{sec:spade_conclusions}
% ==============================================================================

The original low-mode/TQFI intuition correctly identified that spatial-mode structure is the relevant resource.  What failed was not that physical intuition, but the assumption that the present file contained enough outcomes to reconstruct the required reduced state.  The available observable is one noisy aggregate output.  The defensible result is therefore a channel-resolved likelihood model, followed by a conditional QFI and CFI comparison after the state and channel assumptions have been declared and tested.

\begin{remark}[Supported conclusions]
    The current counts require a baseline component and overdispersed noise; a simple ideal HG1 Poisson model is rejected.  The repeated data support constrained likelihood fits and bootstrap tests of complete channel configurations.  Under the reference assumptions, the $F=2.6$ configuration is compatible with the data whereas its $F=1$ counterpart is not.  The validated quantum model shows that complete ideal SPADE is nearly QFI-optimal in the tested regime, but that real binary readout can lose much of this information.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Claims that remain out of scope]
    Aggregate $n_1$ data alone do not separately identify dark counts, incident flux, photon survival, bright-mode leakage, and first-order transmission.  They do not identify HG-basis dephasing, jointly determine source and sorter displacement, reconstruct coherences, or produce a data-derived density matrix or TQFI.  An unconstrained negative tail coefficient is not a negative physical leakage probability, and a favourable bootstrap $p$-value is not proof of a unique apparatus model.
\end{remark}

The most valuable next data products are listed in Table~\ref{tab:spade_calibration_handoff}.  They turn the conditional analysis into a progressively calibrated experiment: every independently measured quantity removes a degeneracy rather than adding another unconstrained fit parameter.

\noindent The proposed future measurement can be summarized as
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
 source and alignment calibration
               |
 triggered acquisition with known normalization
               |
               v
 [multi-output SPADE]
    |--> HG00 counts
    |--> HG01 counts
    |--> HG10 counts
    |--> selected higher-mode counts
    +--> complement / no-click counts
               |
 source-blocked data + mode-injection confusion matrix
               |
               v
 calibrated modal probabilities --> experimental SPADE CFI
               |
 validated state/channel model --> comparison with model QFI
               |
 coherence-sensitive data, if available --> state-level QFI or TQFI
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Reproducibility principle}

The analysis should be archived as an ordered channel configuration together with the count file, the selected likelihood, the bootstrap seed and size, and every fixed calibration constant.  The reproducible workflow is: diagnose baseline and Fano structure; use effective models to reveal missing physics; impose independently justified channel constraints; fit a small identifiable parameter set; bootstrap the complete configuration; and only then compute QFI and SPADE-information fractions for the accepted scenarios.  This is the practical route from the present count cube to an experimentally defensible QFI-informed measurement-design study.
