\section{Broad Context and Motivation: The Second Quantum Revolution}
Over the past century, quantum mechanics has transitioned from a fundamental, often controversial, inquiry into the microscopic laws of nature to a powerful driver of technological innovation, inaugurating the era of the \emph{Second Quantum Revolution}~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. While the first quantum wave yielded transformative solid-state technologies such as the transistor, the laser, and the semiconductor microchip, the current frontier leverages the non-local, phase-sensitive, and coherent properties of individual quantum states—specifically superposition, entanglement, and quantum squeezing—to perform information processing tasks that are fundamentally impossible within the framework of classical physics~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Santamaria2025spectroscopy}.

Within this landscape, \textbf{Quantum Metrology} stands as one of the most mature and impactful applications of quantum information science~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Maccone2020squeezing}.

By preparing the probes in highly entangled states (such as the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger state), quantum metrology unlocks the Heisenberg Limit (HL), compressing the parameter uncertainty scale quadratically to $\mathcal{O}(1/K)$. This dramatic scaling enhancement has revolutionary implications across multiple scales of scientific inquiry, ranging from macroscopic gravitational wave detection to nanoscale quantum sensing, biological microscopy, and high-precision astronomical imaging~\cite{Tsang2016, Santamaria2024single, Maccone2020squeezing}.

\section{The Problem Statement: Computational and Spatial Resolution Gaps}
% ==============================================================================
Despite the \cancel{\text{immense}} \textcolor{blue}{significant} theoretical promise of \cancel{\text{quantum-optimal sensing}} \textcolor{blue}{quantum metrology}, a gap remains between its fundamental precision limits and its practical implementation on NISQ hardware and experimental optical receivers. This thesis addresses three main bottlenecks that arise when applying quantum metrology to near-term quantum sensing and sub-Rayleigh imaging.


\subsection{The Classical Computational Bottleneck of Mixed-State QFI}

{
\color{red} old: 
Evaluating the ultimate metrological utility of a parameterized quantum state $\rho(\theta)$ requires computing its Quantum Fisher Information (QFI). For mixed states under realistic, open-system noise, the exact QFI calculation requires solving the continuous operator Lyapunov equation to retrieve the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) $L_\theta$. Evaluating the spectral decomposition of a density matrix acting on $n$ qubits classically scales as $\mathcal{O}(2^{3n})$ or $\mathcal{O}(8^n)$. As a result, calculating exact ground-truth QFI baselines for intermediate-scale many-body quantum sensors is classically intractable, necessitating the development of scalable, dynamics-agnostic variational algorithms on quantum simulators.
}

{
\color{blue} new:
The first bottleneck concerns the computational cost of evaluating the sensitivity of a quantum state. The maximum sensitivity that a quantum state can provide for estimating an unknown parameter $\theta$ is quantified by the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI). A large QFI means that small changes in the parameter produce changes in the state that are, in principle, easier to detect.
For an ideal pure state, calculating the QFI is relatively manageable. However, real quantum sensors interact with their environment and are generally described by mixed states. A mixed state of \(n\) qubits is represented by a \(2^n\times 2^n\) density matrix. Its size therefore grows exponentially with the number of qubits.
Calculating its exact QFI usually requires finding the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD), an auxiliary operator that describes how the state changes with the parameter. This can be done through a Lyapunov equation or through the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the density matrix. Both approaches become classically expensive because they operate on exponentially large matrices. Standard matrix diagonalization scales as
$\mathcal{O}(2^{3n})$. Thus, even if an intermediate-scale quantum sensor can be physically prepared, calculating its exact QFI on a classical computer may be impossible.

This motivates scalable variational methods that use quantum processors to estimate lower and upper bounds on the QFI without reconstructing and diagonalizing the complete density matrix.
}

\subsection{The Metrological Characterization of Subsystem Noise}

{
\color{red} old: 
While real-world sensors inevitably undergo decoherence, conventional metrological benchmarks often quantify noise strictly in terms of a state's purity or entropy. However, physical noise channels (such as depolarizing decay versus structured thermal Gibbs mixing) introduce highly distinct deformations in the local state manifold. There is a lack of rigorous theoretical and numerical understanding regarding how many-body environmental correlations propagate into a localized subsystem after tracing out the unobserved environment, and how the specific \emph{mechanism of mixedness}—rather than the raw amount of entropy—governs local parameter sensitivity.
}

{
\color{blue} new:
The second bottleneck concerns how environmental noise changes the sensitivity of a quantum sensor. Noise generally transforms a pure state into a mixed state, and its effect is often summarized through the resulting purity or entropy. These quantities measure how mixed the state is, but they do not describe the physical process that produced the mixedness.

Different noise mechanisms can generate states with the same purity while preserving very different amounts of information about the parameter of interest. For example, depolarizing noise mixes a state in a largely unstructured way, whereas thermal Gibbs mixing distributes its population according to the energy levels of the system. Thermal mixing may therefore populate parameter-sensitive excited states, while depolarizing noise may erase the same information. The amount of mixedness alone is consequently not sufficient to predict metrological sensitivity.

This distinction becomes especially important when the sensor is only a small part of a larger many-body system. The inaccessible degrees of freedom are removed by taking a partial trace, but their interactions and correlations can still influence the reduced state of the observed subsystem. A central problem is therefore to understand how these correlations propagate into a local sensor and how the specific \emph{mechanism of mixedness}, rather than only its purity or entropy, determines the local QFI.
}

\subsection{The High-Contrast Resolution Barrier: Rayleigh's Curse}

{
\color{red} old: 
In astronomical observation, the direct detection and spectral characterization of faint exoplanets situated near bright stars is fundamentally limited by diffraction~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. When imaged classically via direct intensity measurements, the point-spread functions (PSFs) of the two sources overlap in the image plane~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. As their spatial separation $d$ falls below the Rayleigh diffraction length $w_0$, the classical Fisher information collapses quadratically as $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$, where $\epsilon \ll 1$ represents the extreme intensity ratio (contrast) of the planet to the star~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This statistical collapse, known as Rayleigh's Curse, causes the estimation uncertainty to diverge, burying the planetary signal under starlight shot noise~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. Although Spatial-Mode Demultiplexing (SPADE) has been proposed to preserve sensitivity down to arbitrary sub-wavelength scales by sorting the field into transverse modes (such as Hermite-Gaussian modes)~\cite{Santamaria2024single}, practical physical setups are heavily degraded by experimental non-idealities, including spatial cross talk, residual centroid misalignments, and atmospheric turbulence~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.
}

{
\color{blue} new:
The third bottleneck arises in astronomical imaging: a telescope does not produce a perfect point-like image of a distant point source. In fact, because of diffraction, a point source is recorded as a finite intensity distribution known as a point-spread function (PSF). When two sources are closer than the characteristic diffraction length, their PSFs strongly overlap in the image plane~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.

This problem is particularly severe for an exoplanet situated near its host star. The planet is both very close to the star and much fainter than it. Their brightness imbalance is described by the contrast parameter $\epsilon\ll 1$. In conventional direct imaging, which records the spatial intensity distribution, small changes in their separation $d$ then produce only very small changes in the measured image. The classical Fisher information available for estimating the separation decreases and, in the high-contrast regime considered here, is strongly suppressed by the small planetary contribution, scaling as $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This loss of statistical sensitivity is commonly known as Rayleigh's curse.

Spatial-Mode Demultiplexing (SPADE) addresses this problem by measuring the spatial structure of the optical field rather than only its image-plane intensity. It separates the incoming light into transverse modes, such as Hermite--Gaussian modes, whose photon populations can retain information about very small source separations~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. In an ideal receiver, this approach can therefore outperform direct imaging in the sub-Rayleigh regime. Its practical performance, however, is affected by experimental imperfections such as spatial cross talk between modes, imperfect alignment with the stellar centroid, and atmospheric turbulence~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The remaining challenge is to determine how much of the theoretical sensitivity survives under realistic operating conditions.
}

\subsection{The Conceptual Divide}
Most critically, there is no existing synthesis that connects the variational, rank-truncated information bounds developed for NISQ quantum simulators to the physical hardware-level projectors utilized in spatial-mode optical receivers. Resolving this conceptual gap would show that spatial mode sorting is not just a classical optics technique, but a physical, analog quantum algorithm that saturates the ultimate variational limits of quantum metrology.
}

{
    \color{blue} new:
\subsection{Connecting the Two Settings}

The three bottlenecks arise in two physically different settings, but the proposed approaches share a common mathematical idea. Variational QFI methods approximate a quantum state by projecting it onto a limited subspace containing its most informative components. Similarly, a SPADE receiver projects an optical field onto a limited set of spatial modes that carry information about the source parameters. The precise relationship between these two forms of projection is not immediately evident and constitutes an additional conceptual question addressed by this thesis. Establishing this connection may provide a common framework for understanding information extraction in both near-term quantum processors and spatial-mode optical receivers.
}

\section{Aims and Objectives}
% ==============================================================================

The central goal of this thesis is to establish a rigorous mathematical, numerical, and experimental bridge between variational quantum metrology on near-term hardware and sub-Rayleigh spatial-mode optical sensing. 
\cancel{\text{To achieve this, we pursue the following specific objectives:}}
\textcolor{blue}{The objectives listed below follow the structure of the thesis. The first two objectives develop the computational metrology track, whereas the third addresses the optical sensing track. These two tracks are largely independent and can be considered separately. The fourth objective brings them together by identifying the mathematical principles they share.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\textcolor{red}{old: Develop a Scalable, NISQ-Friendly Algorithmic Pipeline:}} Implement and validate the Variational Quantum Fisher Information Estimation (VQFIE) protocol, coupled with the Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE). We aim to extract dominant eigenvalues and eigenvectors using a qubit-frugal, single-copy register to compute dynamics-agnostic lower and upper bounds on the QFI, bypassing classical diagonalization bottlenecks.
    \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{new: Develop a scalable algorithmic pipeline for near-term quantum devices:}} The first objective is to implement and validate Variational Quantum Fisher Information Estimation (VQFIE), together with the Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE). These methods are designed for NISQ devices, whose limited number of qubits and sensitivity to noise restrict the complexity of the algorithms that they can run. The VQSE is used to estimate the dominant eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a quantum state using a qubit-efficient, single-copy register. This information is then used by VQFIE to calculate lower and upper bounds on the QFI without requiring the complete classical diagonalization of the state. The resulting bounds do not depend on the particular dynamics used to encode the parameter.

    
    \item \textbf{\textcolor{red}{old: Characterize the Metrological Footprint of Localized Open-System Noise:}} Systematically analyze many-body quantum sensors (modeled under the Transverse-Field Ising Model) under realistic environmental decay channels at the subsystem level. We aim to demonstrate how boundary conditions and low-temperature thermal mixing access sensitive excited states of the Hamiltonian, allowing structured mixed states to outperform pure ground-state subsystems.
    \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{new: Determine how localized open-system noise affects metrological sensitivity:}} The second objective is to study many-body quantum sensors described by the Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM). In particular, we examine how realistic environmental decay processes affect a localized subsystem of a larger quantum sensor. The aim is to determine whether sensitivity depends only on how mixed the subsystem is or also on the physical mechanism that produced this mixedness. We investigate how boundary conditions and low-temperature thermal mixing can populate parameter-sensitive excited states of the Hamiltonian. Under appropriate conditions, these structured mixed states may retain greater sensitivity than subsystems obtained from a pure ground state.

    \item \textbf{\textcolor{red}{old: Implement a High-Contrast sub-Rayleigh Projective Setup:}} Mathematically characterize and experimentally implement a star-centric SPADE tabletop receiver operating in the heralded single-photon regime~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. We aim to evaluate the statistical error-propagation limits for spatial separation $d$ and relative intensity $\epsilon$ in the presence of physical spatial cross talk ($\chi = 0.0035$) and centroid misalignments~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.
    \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{new:Implement a high-contrast sub-Rayleigh optical receiver:}} The third objective begins a separate, experimental track focused on optical sensing. We mathematically characterize and experimentally implement a tabletop receiver based on SPADE for the detection of a faint source located close to a much brighter star~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The receiver operates in the heralded single-photon regime and uses projections onto selected spatial modes to recover information that is difficult to access through direct imaging. We evaluate the statistical uncertainty in the estimation of the source separation $d$ and relative intensity $\epsilon$. The analysis includes experimentally relevant imperfections, particularly spatial crosstalk between the measured modes, quantified by $\chi = 0.0035$, and misalignment with respect to the source centroid~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.
    

    \item \textbf{\textcolor{red}{Construct the Unified 2-Basis Projection Bridge:}} Mathematically prove that the physical alignment and mode-sorting subroutines of SPADE are the exact physical, analog equivalents of the VQSE majorization loop and the rank-truncation projection operator $\Pi^{(1)}$ developed in our NISQ metrology track. We aim to demonstrate that the variational bounds optimized on NISQ simulators are the physical limits governing sub-diffraction exoplanet detection and spectroscopy.
   \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{Establish a unified two-basis projection framework:}} As a final objective, we compare the spectral projection used in VQFIE with the spatial-mode projection implemented by SPADE. More specifically, we investigate how the alignment and mode-sorting operations of the optical receiver relate to the majorization objective of the VQSE and to the rank-truncation projector $\Pi^{(1)}$. This connection is established under the physical assumptions relevant to the optical model, rather than by treating the two procedures as identical in every respect. The aim is to determine how the variational information bounds developed for NISQ simulations can help describe the precision limits of sub-diffraction exoplanet detection and spectroscopy.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
% ==============================================================================

The scope and principal limitations of this work are defined below. These boundaries specify the systems, estimation problems, and experimental conditions considered in the thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Many-Body Systems:} Our numerical simulations of open-system metrology are restricted to 1D spin chains governed by the Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM) up to $N=6$ sites. We do not attempt to simulate macroscopic material phases or high-temperature superconducting systems.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Bounds:} Our parameter estimation framework focuses on single-parameter sensing (e.g., transverse magnetic field strength $h_x$ and spatial separation $d$). Multiparameter scenarios are evaluated under decoupling limits (such as high-contrast exoplanet alignment) rather than simultaneous saturation of the Holevo bound across incompatible measurements.
    \item \textbf{Optical Regime:} The experimental and analytical treatments of SPADE operate within the weak-field, faint-signal limit (single-photon regime) where multi-photon coincidence statistics are negligible. The spatial modes are truncated to the first three Hermite-Gaussian components ($HG_{00}, HG_{01}, HG_{10}$). We do not model infinite-dimensional, arbitrary spatial-field configurations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations of Quantum Estimation and Information Geometry}
This chapter establishes the mathematical language used throughout the thesis. It begins with the distinction between intrinsic quantum (ontic) indeterminacy and classical statistical (epistemic) ignorance, then introduces mixed states, channels, and Positive operator-valued measures (POVM)s. It derives the classical Fisher information, the SLD definition of mixed-state QFI, and the spectral and vectorized formulas used for the numerical reference calculations. It then connects fidelity and the Bures metric to QFI bounds, states the quantum Cram\'{e}r--Rao bound, and distinguishes physical QFI from the ansatz metric used in quantum natural-gradient optimization. Generalized measurements, statistical-distance geometry, and multiparameter extensions are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:mathematical_background}.

\subsection*{Chapter 3: Variational Quantum Sensing and Optimization on NISQ Hardware}
Here, we present our scalable quantum-classical hybrid pipeline to estimate and optimize mixed-state metrology bounds on near-term hardware. We define the 1D Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM) and our mixed-state generation protocol using the partial trace. We introduce the Truncated Quantum Fisher Information (TQFI) lower and upper bounds (based on the generalized subnormalized Uhlmann fidelity $F_*$) and the Sub/Super-Fidelity QFI (SSQFI) bounds based on polynomial trace functionals. We detail the Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE) and the Variational Quantum Fisher Information Estimation (VQFIE) algorithms, detailing how VQSE leverages quantum majorization and a local-to-global adaptive Hamiltonian schedule to diagonalize states on single-copy registers while bypassing barren plateaus.

\subsection*{Chapter 4: Open-System Noise Metrology: Fingerprints of Mixedness at the Subsystem Level}
This chapter investigates how environmental noise corrupts the metrological sensitivity of localized quantum sensors under open-system conditions. We benchmark six state families (including global and reduced depolarizing and thermal Gibbs states) under two operating modes: fixed-noise and matched-purity. We derive the matched-SLD analytical local derivative correction, which incorporates field-dependent chain-rule coordinates via the Duhamel formula, resolving non-physical monotonicity violations of earlier secant-like approximations. Through a systematic evaluation of five experimental presets, we analyze critical peak collapses, spatial peak migrations, and boundary condition sensitivities, proving that structured thermal states can preserve superior local sensitivity relative to ground-state subsystems.

\subsection*{Chapter 5: Astrophysical Metrology: Rayleigh's Curse and the Exoplanet Detection Challenge}
We transition the parameter-estimation framework to high-contrast astronomical imaging~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. After defining the exoplanet problem and the Rayleigh collapse of direct imaging, we develop the mixed-state optical model and the low-order HG SPADE receiver. This chapter also contains the receiver-level error propagation, calibration, sequential channel model, maximum-likelihood fit, and overdispersed-count validation of the tabletop experiment.

\subsection*{Chapter 6: Bridging Variational Metrology and Sub-Rayleigh Quantum Imaging: The 2-Basis Projection Framework}
This chapter compares the spectral truncation used in VQFIE with the low-order HG modal cutoff used by SPADE. It makes the required weak-source, high-contrast, Gaussian, and aligned-basis assumptions explicit; relates star-centric alignment to the VQSE objective without identifying it with full VQSE diagonalization; and develops the star-centric QFIM transformation and high-contrast decoupling argument. The experimental apparatus and validation are deliberately kept in Section \ref{sec:astrophysical_experiment}.

\subsection*{Chapter 7: Conclusions, Synthesis, and Scientific Outlook}
This final chapter summarizes the core scientific achievements of the thesis, synthesizing the computational, theoretical, and experimental insights gained across both tracks. We discuss the broader implications of our unified projection framework for near-term quantum technologies and outline future research directions, including multiparameter adaptive astronomical arrays, squeezed-light enhanced SPADE, and hardware implementations of VQSE-assisted sensors on physical NISQ processors.
