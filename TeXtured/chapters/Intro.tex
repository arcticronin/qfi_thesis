\section{Broad Context and Motivation: The Second Quantum Revolution}
Over the past century, quantum mechanics has transitioned from a fundamental, often controversial, inquiry into the microscopic laws of nature to a powerful driver of technological innovation, inaugurating the era of the \emph{Second Quantum Revolution}~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. While the first quantum wave yielded transformative solid-state technologies such as the transistor, the laser, and the semiconductor microchip, the current frontier leverages the non-local, phase-sensitive, and coherent properties of individual quantum states—specifically superposition, entanglement, and quantum squeezing—to perform information processing tasks that are fundamentally impossible within the framework of classical physics~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Santamaria2025spectroscopy}.

Within this landscape, \textbf{Quantum Metrology} stands as one of the most mature and impactful applications of quantum information science~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Maccone2020squeezing}.

Quantum metrology is the science of performing highly precise measurements of physical quantities by exploiting the principles of quantum mechanics. In particular, it uses quantum resources such as entanglement and squeezed states of light to estimate parameters—including magnetic fields, temporal phase shifts, optical paths, and spatial separations—with a precision that can surpass the fundamental limits of classical statistical estimation~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Santamaria2025spectroscopy}. In classical estimation, independent measurement probes are bound by the Standard Quantum Limit (SQL), where the statistical uncertainty scales as $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{K})$ for $K$ probes. By preparing the probes in highly entangled states (such as the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger state), quantum metrology unlocks the Heisenberg Limit (HL), compressing the parameter uncertainty scale quadratically to $\mathcal{O}(1/K)$. This dramatic scaling enhancement has revolutionary implications across multiple scales of scientific inquiry, ranging from macroscopic gravitational wave detection to nanoscale quantum sensing, biological microscopy, and high-precision astronomical imaging~\cite{Tsang2016, Santamaria2024single, Maccone2020squeezing}.

Two examples that are particularly important for this thesis are near-term quantum processors and astronomical optical sensing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) Devices:}
    Modern quantum processors contain a moderate number of qubits ($10$ to $1000$), but they are still affected by physical imperfections that corrupt quantum information and operations, including environmental decoherence, gate errors, and imperfectly characterized state decay. Quantum metrology provides a framework for studying and optimizing the sensitivity of these devices using scalable quantum--classical algorithms.

    \item \textbf{Astronomical and Optical Sensing:}
    Quantum metrology can also improve the extraction of information from light. In astronomy, for example, diffraction makes it difficult to distinguish objects that appear very close together, such as a faint exoplanet next to its bright host star. Spatial-mode optical receivers separate incoming light according to its spatial structure, allowing information about closely spaced objects to be recovered more effectively than with conventional imaging. This techinque is usually known as sub-Rayleigh imaging.~\cite{Santamaria2024single, Santamaria2025spectroscopy}.
\end{enumerate}

Although these two settings involve very different physical systems, they share the same underlying goal: extracting as much information as possible about an unknown parameter from a quantum state. They therefore provide two complementary examples of how quantum metrology can move from an abstract theory of precision to practical sensing methods.

\section{The Problem Statement: Computational and Spatial Resolution Gaps}
% ==============================================================================
Despite the significant theoretical promise of quantum metrology, a gap remains between its fundamental precision limits and its practical implementation on NISQ hardware and experimental optical receivers. This thesis addresses three main bottlenecks that arise when applying quantum metrology to near-term quantum sensing and sub-Rayleigh imaging.


\subsection{The Classical Computational Bottleneck of Mixed-State QFI}

The first bottleneck concerns the computational cost of evaluating the sensitivity of a quantum state. The maximum sensitivity that a quantum state can provide for estimating an unknown parameter $\theta$ is quantified by the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI). A large QFI means that small changes in the parameter produce changes in the state that are, in principle, easier to detect.
For an ideal pure state, calculating the QFI is relatively manageable. However, real quantum sensors interact with their environment and are generally described by mixed states. A mixed state of \(n\) qubits is represented by a \(2^n\times 2^n\) density matrix. Its size therefore grows exponentially with the number of qubits.
Calculating its exact QFI usually requires finding the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD), an auxiliary operator that describes how the state changes with the parameter. This can be done through a Lyapunov equation or through the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the density matrix. Both approaches become classically expensive because they operate on exponentially large matrices. Standard matrix diagonalization scales as
$\mathcal{O}(2^{3n})$. Thus, even if an intermediate-scale quantum sensor can be physically prepared, calculating its exact QFI on a classical computer may be impossible.

This motivates scalable variational methods that use quantum processors to estimate lower and upper bounds on the QFI without reconstructing and diagonalizing the complete density matrix.


\subsection{The Metrological Characterization of Subsystem Noise}

The second bottleneck concerns how environmental noise changes the sensitivity of a quantum sensor. Noise generally transforms a pure state into a mixed state, and its effect is often summarized through the resulting purity or entropy. These quantities measure how mixed the state is, but they do not describe the physical process that produced the mixedness.

Different noise mechanisms can generate states with the same purity while preserving very different amounts of information about the parameter of interest. For example, depolarizing noise mixes a state in a largely unstructured way, whereas thermal Gibbs mixing distributes its population according to the energy levels of the system. Thermal mixing may therefore populate parameter-sensitive excited states, while depolarizing noise may erase the same information. The amount of mixedness alone is consequently not sufficient to predict metrological sensitivity.

This distinction becomes especially important when the sensor is only a small part of a larger many-body system. The inaccessible degrees of freedom are removed by taking a partial trace, but their interactions and correlations can still influence the reduced state of the observed subsystem. A central problem is therefore to understand how these correlations propagate into a local sensor and how the specific \emph{mechanism of mixedness}, rather than only its purity or entropy, determines the local QFI.

\subsection{The High-Contrast Resolution Barrier: Rayleigh's Curse}

The third bottleneck arises in astronomical imaging: a telescope does not produce a perfect point-like image of a distant point source. In fact, because of diffraction, a point source is recorded as a finite intensity distribution known as a point-spread function (PSF). When two sources are closer than the characteristic diffraction length, their PSFs strongly overlap in the image plane~\cite{Santamaria2024single}.

This problem is particularly severe for an exoplanet situated near its host star. The planet is both very close to the star and much fainter than it. Their brightness imbalance is described by the contrast parameter $\epsilon\ll 1$. In conventional direct imaging, which records the spatial intensity distribution, small changes in their separation $d$ then produce only very small changes in the measured image. The classical Fisher information available for estimating the separation decreases and, in the high-contrast regime considered here, is strongly suppressed by the small planetary contribution, scaling as $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)$~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. This loss of statistical sensitivity is commonly known as Rayleigh's curse.

Spatial-Mode Demultiplexing (SPADE) addresses this problem by measuring the spatial structure of the optical field rather than only its image-plane intensity. It separates the incoming light into transverse modes, such as Hermite--Gaussian modes, whose photon populations can retain information about very small source separations~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. In an ideal receiver, this approach can therefore outperform direct imaging in the sub-Rayleigh regime. Its practical performance, however, is affected by experimental imperfections such as spatial cross talk between modes, imperfect alignment with the stellar centroid, and atmospheric turbulence~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. The remaining challenge is to determine how much of the theoretical sensitivity survives under realistic operating conditions.

\subsection{Connecting the Two Settings}

The two settings share a concern with retaining useful information under limited access, but their reductions are physically different. Variational QFI methods retain a dominant state eigenspace, whereas SPADE measures a fixed spatial-mode basis. The practical optical analysis therefore keeps these constructions distinct: it uses low-order modes as experimentally meaningful measurement outcomes and does not identify them automatically with the spectral truncation used by VQFIE.

\section{Aims and Objectives}
% ==============================================================================

The central goal of this thesis is to develop and apply QFI-based reasoning in two complementary settings: variational metrology on near-term quantum hardware and sub-Rayleigh spatial-mode optical sensing. The first two objectives develop the computational metrology track, whereas the remaining objectives establish and analyse the optical experiment.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item 
    \textbf{Develop a scalable algorithmic pipeline for near-term quantum devices:} The first objective is to implement and validate Variational Quantum Fisher Information Estimation (VQFIE), together with the Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE). These methods are designed for NISQ devices, whose limited number of qubits and sensitivity to noise restrict the complexity of the algorithms that they can run. The VQSE is used to estimate the dominant eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a quantum state using a qubit-efficient, single-copy register. This information is then used by VQFIE to calculate lower and upper bounds on the QFI without requiring the complete classical diagonalization of the state. The resulting bounds do not depend on the particular dynamics used to encode the parameter.

    
    \item
    \textbf{Determine how localized open-system noise affects metrological sensitivity:} The second objective is to study many-body quantum sensors described by the Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM). In particular, we examine how realistic environmental decay processes affect a localized subsystem of a larger quantum sensor. The aim is to determine whether sensitivity depends only on how mixed the subsystem is or also on the physical mechanism that produced this mixedness. We investigate how boundary conditions and low-temperature thermal mixing can populate parameter-sensitive excited states of the Hamiltonian. Under appropriate conditions, these structured mixed states may retain greater sensitivity than subsystems obtained from a pure ground state.

    \item
    \textbf{Establish the high-contrast sub-Rayleigh imaging problem and SPADE baseline:} The third objective begins the optical-sensing track. We formulate the detection of a faint source near a much brighter star~\cite{Santamaria2024single}, explain the poor high-contrast scaling of direct imaging, and introduce a low-order SPADE receiver. This supplies the physical source and measurement model needed to interpret the recorded spatial-mode counts.
    

    \item
   \textbf{Develop a channel-resolved analysis of the SPADE record:} The final objective is to determine what the available repeated aggregate first-order counts can support. We distinguish an attractive but unavailable data-derived TQFI construction from the analysis that the record does support: an explicit sequence of loss, alignment, dephasing, imperfect-POVM, and classical count-noise components. This permits conditional likelihood and bootstrap tests of calibrated channel configurations, followed by clearly labelled model-QFI and CFI comparisons.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
% ==============================================================================

The scope and principal limitations of this work are defined below. These boundaries specify the systems, estimation problems, and experimental conditions considered in the thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Many-Body Systems:} Our numerical simulations of open-system metrology are restricted to 1D spin chains governed by the Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM) up to $N=6$ sites. We do not attempt to simulate macroscopic material phases or high-temperature superconducting systems.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Bounds:} Our parameter estimation framework focuses on single-parameter sensing (e.g., transverse magnetic field strength $h_x$ and spatial separation $d$). Multiparameter scenarios are evaluated under decoupling limits (such as high-contrast exoplanet alignment) rather than simultaneous saturation of the Holevo bound across incompatible measurements.
    \item \textbf{Optical Regime:} The experimental and analytical treatments of SPADE operate within the weak-field, faint-signal limit (single-photon regime) where multi-photon coincidence statistics are negligible. The physical record is an aggregate first-order count, while the QFI model uses a controlled finite HG representation with an unresolved tail. We do not reconstruct an arbitrary infinite-dimensional optical state from the counts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations of Quantum Estimation and Information Geometry}
This chapter establishes the mathematical language used throughout the thesis. It begins with the distinction between intrinsic quantum (ontic) indeterminacy and classical statistical (epistemic) ignorance, then introduces mixed states, channels, and Positive operator-valued measures (POVM)s. It derives the classical Fisher information, the SLD definition of mixed-state QFI, and the spectral and vectorized formulas used for the numerical reference calculations. It then connects fidelity and the Bures metric to QFI bounds, states the quantum Cram\'{e}r--Rao bound, and distinguishes physical QFI from the ansatz metric used in quantum natural-gradient optimization. Generalized measurements, statistical-distance geometry, and multiparameter extensions are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:mathematical_background}.

\subsection*{Chapter 3: Variational Quantum Sensing and Optimization on NISQ Hardware}
Here, we present our scalable quantum-classical hybrid pipeline to estimate and optimize mixed-state metrology bounds on near-term hardware. We define the 1D Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM) and our mixed-state generation protocol using the partial trace. We introduce the Truncated Quantum Fisher Information (TQFI) lower and upper bounds (based on the generalized subnormalized Uhlmann fidelity $F_*$) and the Sub/Super-Fidelity QFI (SSQFI) bounds based on polynomial trace functionals. We detail the Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE) and the Variational Quantum Fisher Information Estimation (VQFIE) algorithms, detailing how VQSE leverages quantum majorization and a local-to-global adaptive Hamiltonian schedule to diagonalize states on single-copy registers while bypassing barren plateaus.

\subsection*{Chapter 4: Open-System Noise Metrology: Fingerprints of Mixedness at the Subsystem Level}
This chapter investigates how environmental noise corrupts the metrological sensitivity of localized quantum sensors under open-system conditions. We benchmark six state families (including global and reduced depolarizing and thermal Gibbs states) under two operating modes: fixed-noise and matched-purity. We derive the matched-SLD analytical local derivative correction, which incorporates field-dependent chain-rule coordinates via the Duhamel formula, resolving non-physical monotonicity violations of earlier secant-like approximations. Through a systematic evaluation of five experimental presets, we analyze critical peak collapses, spatial peak migrations, and boundary condition sensitivities, proving that structured thermal states can preserve superior local sensitivity relative to ground-state subsystems.

\subsection*{Chapter 5: High-Contrast Sub-Rayleigh Imaging}
We transition the parameter-estimation framework to high-contrast astronomical imaging~\cite{Santamaria2024single}. After defining the exoplanet problem and the Rayleigh collapse of direct imaging, this chapter develops the weak-light optical model and the low-order HG SPADE receiver. It establishes the physical baseline without anticipating the later channel fit or QFI analysis.

\subsection*{Chapter 6: Practical SPADE: Channel Modelling and Conditional QFI Analysis}
This chapter begins with the initially intended accessible-mode/TQFI analysis and explains why an aggregate first-order count record cannot implement it. It then develops the sequential source, loss, alignment, dephasing, imperfect-POVM, and count-noise model; diagnoses the measured baseline and overdispersion; and performs conditional likelihood and bootstrap tests. QFI and CFI comparisons are used as model-validation and measurement-design tools, with their assumptions and limits stated explicitly.

\subsection*{Chapter 7: Conclusions and Outlook}
This final chapter summarizes the computational and optical-sensing results, distinguishing exact QFI benchmarks, variational bounds, and the conditional information quantities of the practical SPADE model. It also identifies the calibration data required to turn the present count-channel analysis into a fully calibrated multi-mode information experiment.
