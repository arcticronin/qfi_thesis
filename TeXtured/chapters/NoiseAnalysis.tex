\chapter{Quantum Metrology under Noise}
\label{ch:noise_analysis}

This chapter studies a simple practical question: if an $N$-qubit probe encodes the transverse field $h_x$, how much information about $h_x$ remains when an observer can access only $n$ of its qubits?  We call the accessible subsystem $S$ and the inaccessible complement $E$.  The observer's state is obtained by discarding $E$.

The central issue is therefore \emph{local metrology}.  Quantum Fisher information (QFI) measures how rapidly a parameterized quantum state changes when $h_x$ changes: more local QFI means that, in principle, measurements on $S$ can estimate $h_x$ more precisely.  We ask not only how much QFI is lost by restricted access, but also whether the answer depends on \emph{how the full probe was prepared} before $E$ was discarded.

The numerical framework follows Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, QFI is evaluated with the formalism of Chapter~\ref{ch:MathBackground}, and the finite-displacement fidelity quantities follow Section~\ref{sec:bounds}.  The preparation history matters.  A ground state, a globally Gibbs-prepared state, and a depolarized state can all yield mixed states on $S$, but they do not represent the same physical experiment or the same parameterized state family.

\begin{remark}[What this chapter establishes]
    For the finite TFIM geometries, field windows, and state families sampled here, local QFI grows strongly when more qubits are accessible.  Depolarization supplies a clean unstructured-noise baseline and lowers local QFI monotonically as its strength grows.  By contrast, global Gibbs preparation at a fixed temperature can leave \emph{more} local QFI than the reduced ground-state family in selected regimes.  This is not a claim that a fixed thermal-noise channel increases QFI.  None of the results is a universal scaling law or a statement that temperature is generically beneficial.
\end{remark}

\section{The Physical Question and the State Families}
\label{sec:noise_state_families}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection{One probe, restricted access}

Let $S$ denote the retained $n$-qubit subsystem and $E$ the discarded complement of $N-n$ qubits.  The parameter $h_x$ is the estimation parameter $\theta$ of Chapters~\ref{ch:MathBackground} and~\ref{ch:methods}, its notation is retained to match the numerical data sets.  Mathematically, discarding $E$ means applying the partial trace $\operatorname{Tr}_E$.  Physically, it means that the observer is unable to measure those qubits.  It is the same restricted-access operation in every branch of this chapter.

\begin{infobox}{Reader's map: what is being compared}
    Every comparison starts from the same parameter-dependent TFIM Hamiltonian $H_N(h_x)$ and retains the same subsystem $S$.  What changes is the \emph{global preparation} before the partial trace:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Ground-state baseline.}  Prepare the full probe in its ground state and discard $E$.  The resulting $S$ state can already be mixed, because $S$ and $E$ may be entangled.
        \item \textbf{Depolarizing control.}  Prepare the ground state, apply a fixed depolarizing channel to the full probe, and then discard $E$.  This is the chapter's genuine parameter-independent noise channel.
        \item \textbf{Thermal family.}  Prepare the full probe in the Gibbs state at fixed inverse temperature $\beta$, and then discard $E$.  This is a different parameter-dependent preparation family, not a fixed channel applied to the ground-state family.
    \end{enumerate}
    Thus, the main comparison is not ``the same state with two kinds of noise.''  It is a comparison of three local states produced by three distinct histories.  The discarded subsystem $E$ is not the external bath used to prepare a Gibbs state, it is simply the finite part of the probe to which the observer has no access.
\end{infobox}

\begin{definition}[Prepared and reduced state families]
    The ground-state reference, depolarized, and thermal families are
    \begin{align}
        \rho_{\mathrm{G},N}(h_x)
         & \coloneqq |\psi_0(h_x)\rangle\langle\psi_0(h_x)|,               &
        \rho_{\mathrm{sub}}(h_x)
         & \coloneqq \operatorname{Tr}_E[\rho_{\mathrm{G},N}(h_x)]           \\
        \rho_{\mathrm{dep},N}(h_x;P)
         & \coloneqq \Lambda^{(N)}_P[\rho_{\mathrm{G},N}(h_x)],            &
        \rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}(h_x;P)
         & \coloneqq \operatorname{Tr}_E[\rho_{\mathrm{dep},N}(h_x;P)]       \\
        \rho_{\mathrm{th},N}(h_x;\beta)
         & \coloneqq \frac{\exp[-\beta H_N(h_x)]}{Z(h_x,\beta)},           &
        Z(h_x,\beta)
         & \coloneqq \operatorname{Tr}[\exp[-\beta H_N(h_x)]]                \\
        \rho_{\mathrm{th},S}(h_x;\beta)
         & \coloneqq \operatorname{Tr}_E[\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}(h_x;\beta)].
        \label{eq:noise_state_families}
    \end{align}
    Here $\Lambda^{(N)}_P$ is a full-system depolarizing channel with fixed probability $P$, and $\beta$ is the inverse temperature of the Gibbs preparation: larger $\beta$ means a colder Gibbs state, with the ground-state limit recovered only in the appropriate low-temperature limit.
\end{definition}

The three primary local comparators are $\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}$, $\rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}$, and $\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}$.  They have the same accessible Hilbert-space dimension, but not the same spectrum or $h_x$ dependence.  Full-system curves are reference ceilings and consistency checks, the main physical question is always what an observer restricted to $S$ can learn.

\begin{infobox}{Scope of the Thermal Model}
    The thermal branch does not model dynamical thermalization of the accessible subsystem by the discarded qubits. Instead, we assume that the full finite TFIM probe has been prepared in the Gibbs state
    \[
        \rho_{\mathrm{th},N}(h_x;\beta)
        =
        \frac{e^{-\beta H_N(h_x)}}{Z(h_x,\beta)}
    \]
    by an external, unmodelled equilibrium-preparation mechanism. We then restrict access to the subsystem $S$ by tracing out the complementary degrees of freedom $E$.

    Thus, $E$ represents an inaccessible part of the prepared probe, not a macroscopic thermal bath. In general,
    \[
        \operatorname{Tr}_E[\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}]
        \neq
        \frac{e^{-\beta H_S}}{\operatorname{Tr}[e^{-\beta H_S}]},
    \]
    because interactions across the $S$--$E$ boundary and their correlations remain encoded in the reduced state. The present model therefore studies the local metrological consequences of global thermal preparation, rather than a microscopic thermalization dynamics.
\end{infobox}


\subsection{What can be compared directly}

For a fixed $P$, the depolarizing channel and the partial trace are parameter-independent channels.  QFI cannot increase under either operation, so the valid full-to-local comparison is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}(h_x;P)]
    \leq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{dep},N}(h_x;P)].
    \label{eq:depolarizing_data_processing}
\end{equation}
Likewise, restricted access cannot increase the QFI within the ground-state and thermal families:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}(h_x)]
    \leq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{G},N}(h_x)],
    \qquad
    \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}(h_x;\beta)]
    \leq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}(h_x;\beta)].
    \label{eq:local_global_data_processing}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[A necessary distinction]
    It is tempting, but incorrect, to describe the thermal branch as ``the ground state after thermal noise.''  At fixed $\beta$, the Gibbs state is constructed from the parameter-dependent Hamiltonian $H_N(h_x)$, so both its eigenvectors and its level populations vary with $h_x$.  Thermal preparation is therefore not generally a parameter-independent channel applied to the ground-state family.  Consequently, there is no theorem requiring
    \[
        \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}(h_x;\beta)]
        \leq \mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}(h_x)].
    \]
    If the thermal local QFI exceeds the reduced-ground-state local QFI, the two sides are distinct encoded families.  The correct consistency check is instead $\mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}]\leq\mathcal{F}_Q[\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}]$, which is satisfied by the calculations in this chapter.  There is no data-processing violation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fixed preparation parameters and matched-purity control}

Most results use a fixed physical control throughout the $h_x$ sweep: fixed $P$ in the depolarizing branch, or fixed $\beta$ in the thermal branch.  The former is a fixed noise channel, the latter is a fixed temperature used to define a Gibbs-prepared family.  Keeping these controls fixed is the primary physical comparison.

Project~9 also contains a different, deliberately artificial control in which the local purities are matched at every field value:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}^2(h_x;P(h_x))]
    =\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}^2(h_x)],
    \qquad
    \operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}^2(h_x;\beta(h_x))]
    =\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}^2(h_x)].
    \label{eq:purity_matching_cond}
\end{equation}
This asks whether equal local purity is sufficient to imply equal local QFI.  It is a structural control, not a fixed physical-channel experiment, because $P$ and $\beta$ are selected as functions of $h_x$.  In other words, it compares carefully chosen paths through state space rather than one apparatus operated at fixed settings.

\begin{remark}[Derivative along the matched path]
    The SLD must follow the parameterized path actually being compared. For the matched depolarizing family,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}}{\mathrm{d}h_x}
        =(1-P)\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}}{\partial h_x}
        -\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}h_x}\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}
        +\frac{1}{2^n}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}h_x}\mathbb{I}_S,
        \label{eq:matched_depolarizing_derivative}
    \end{equation}
    and the thermal derivative includes
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}}{\mathrm{d}h_x}
        =\operatorname{Tr}_E\!\left[
            \frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}}{\partial h_x}
            +\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{th},N}}{\partial\beta}
            \frac{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathrm{d}h_x}\right].
        \label{eq:matched_thermal_derivative}
    \end{equation}
    Omitting these chain-rule terms would calculate the QFI of a different family. The current Project~9 implementation includes them, and the full-to-local checks provide a practical validation of the corrected derivatives.
\end{remark}

\section{Observables, Validation, and Data Provenance}
\label{sec:noise_observables}
% ==============================================================================

For each value of $h_x$, the calculation constructs each state family, discards $E$, and then asks how strongly the resulting local state changes with $h_x$.  Exact local SLD QFI is the primary answer to this question.  We also evaluate fidelity quantities from $\rho(h_x)$ and $\rho(h_x+\delta h_x)$ because these are the quantities that a truncation-based metrology protocol would access.  The exact calculation and central-difference convention are those of Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}, the finite-displacement functional is Eq.~\eqref{eq:qfi_difference_quotient}.

\begin{remark}[Why SLD QFI and TQFI are reported separately]
    Exact SLD QFI is a local, infinitesimal sensitivity and supports the physical claims in this chapter.  TQFI is instead a finite-displacement, truncation-aware proxy.  At the selected finite displacements, \texttt{reduced\_lower\_tqfi} can exceed exact local SLD QFI: the largest observed excesses are $0.112$ in the latest Project~1 size sweep, $1.068$ in the Project~1 subsystem-size sweep, and $1.071$ in the open-boundary Project~7 data. In contrast, \texttt{reduced\_sub\_qfi\_bound} never exceeds local SLD QFI in the current arrays.  TQFI tightness is therefore itself a methodological result, rather than a substitute for exact local SLD QFI.
\end{remark}

The data set consists of saved \texttt{.npz} outputs generated between 2026-08-22 and 2026-08-25: \texttt{project1\_scaling\_N\_20260822\_162213.npz} and \texttt{project1\_scaling\_n\_20260822\_162301.npz}, six Project~7 outputs dated 2026-08-23, and twenty-one Project~9 outputs dated 2026-08-25. Projects~1 and~7 use $\delta h_x=0.1$ and $m=2$. Project~9 uses $\delta h_x=0.05$ and normally $m=3$. Exact SLD values may be compared when the physical families agree, but raw TQFI magnitudes should not be pooled across projects without accounting for the different displacement and truncation settings.

\section{Project 7: Global Gibbs Preparation and Temperature Dependence}
\label{sec:project7_noise}
% ==============================================================================

Project~7 isolates the thermal family.  For every value of $h_x$, the \emph{whole} $N$-qubit probe is constructed in the Gibbs state at a chosen inverse temperature $\beta$, and the same subsystem $S$ is then retained.  This is not a simulation in which the reduced ground-state probe is coupled to a bath for a finite time.  Rather, it compares the local state produced by global Gibbs preparation with the local state produced by ground-state preparation.

The sampled inverse temperatures are
\[
    \beta=0.1,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 2,\ 5,\ 10,\ 25,\ 50.
\]
Larger $\beta$ corresponds to a colder Gibbs state.  The runner stores $\beta_\star$: the interpolated value at which peak \texttt{reduced\_lower\_tqfi} reaches $90\%$ of the ground-state \texttt{lower\_tqfi} peak.

\begin{remark}[Meaning of $\beta_\star$]
    $\beta_\star$ is an operational finite-displacement recovery scale, not a thermodynamic transition temperature.
\end{remark}

This operational ``recovery'' describes the observed response curve, it does not mean that a fixed noisy channel reverses its own information loss.

\begin{remark}[Physical intuition and caution]
    Thermal QFI can receive contributions from both eigenvector changes and field-dependent populations of low-lying excitations. Around the open-chain peak at $h_x=0.547$, the first excitation gap is approximately $0.038$, even $\beta=50$ is therefore not uniformly in the ground-state regime over the whole scan. The results demonstrate a finite-temperature spectral-susceptibility effect, not that the Gibbs state is more informative than the ground state at every fixed field as $\beta\rightarrow\infty$.
\end{remark}

\section{Project 9: Comparing Preparation Histories under Restricted Access}
\label{sec:project9_noise}
% ==============================================================================

Project~9 is the main controlled comparison.  It places $\rho_{\mathrm{sub}}$, $\rho_{\mathrm{dep},S}$, and $\rho_{\mathrm{th},S}$ on equal $n$-qubit footing: the observer retains the same size subsystem, while the global preparation history changes.  Exact local SLD QFI is the primary output.  The corresponding full-system curves are not competing targets, they are diagnostic references that check that discarding $E$ has not apparently created QFI.  All latest arrays satisfy the full-to-local checks in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:depolarizing_data_processing} and~\eqref{eq:local_global_data_processing}.

The presets answer different questions.  Preset~A checks the matched-purity construction, Preset~B asks how the three local families change as access to the probe grows, Preset~C changes the boundary-condition-dependent spectrum, Preset~D isolates the fixed depolarizing channel, and Preset~E is the central temperature sweep of the globally Gibbs-prepared family.

\section{Synthesis, Limitations, and Research Takeaways}
\label{sec:noise_synthesis}
% ==============================================================================

\begin{infobox}{Plain-language answer}
    Restricted access is always costly within a given preparation family: if some qubits are discarded, an observer using only $S$ cannot have more QFI than an observer using the full corresponding probe.  Depolarization is the simple control case, and it lowers the local QFI as its strength grows.  Global Gibbs preparation is different: it creates a different $h_x$-dependent state before the same qubits are discarded.  In selected finite-system regimes, its low-energy populations carry enough additional $h_x$ dependence that the remaining subsystem has more QFI than the subsystem obtained from the global ground state.

    This does not mean that a thermal bath has added information to one fixed state.  It means that the preparation history determines which information is present before the observer loses access to $E$.
\end{infobox}

\begin{remark}[Supported conclusion: accessible degrees of freedom]
    Projects~1 and~9 show that critical metrological response is spatially distributed. Partial trace removes a variable fraction of it, and the retained fraction grows strongly with accessible subsystem size. Keeping all but one qubit recovers essentially all of the full-system peak QFI in the tested $N=8$ open-chain and $N=6$ periodic cases.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Supported conclusion: the origin of mixedness matters]
    Entanglement-induced mixedness, depolarization, and thermal population are not interchangeable. Depolarization is Hamiltonian blind and monotonically damaging in the sampled sweep. Thermal preparation changes how low-lying levels encode $h_x$ and can be favorable in selected field and boundary-condition regimes. Purity alone does not predict local QFI.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Supported conclusion: boundary conditions are a spectral-control resource]
    Boundary conditions change finite-size gaps, low-energy sectors, peak positions, and thermal recovery scales. In the present systems, they can reverse the ordering between reduced ground-state and reduced thermal families.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Claims that need qualification]
    The present data do not support a universal QFI scaling exponent. Thermal preparation is not generically beneficial and should never be described as a fixed channel increasing QFI of the same encoded family. Local TQFI should not be compared directly with global SLD QFI, and \texttt{lower\_tqfi} should not be called a strict pointwise SLD lower bound at the present finite displacements. Finally, a peak at the edge of the field scan is a reason to validate the scan window, not a final peak location.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Methodological takeaway]
    Exact local SLD QFI is the correct benchmark where it can be computed. Finite-displacement TQFI adds a complementary operational layer by testing whether a truncated or fidelity-based description retains the same feature. Such quantities should be validated against exact local SLD on small systems before they are used where exact SLD is unavailable.
\end{remark}

    \item Save both the present operational $\beta_\star$ and a crossover based on exact local SLD or \texttt{sub\_qfi\_bound}.

\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Practical design principle]
    For finite quantum sensors, local metrological performance is determined by the full sequence that produces the accessible state. Preserve access to the relevant subsystem, engineer the low-energy spectrum, and distinguish structured thermal preparation from structureless external mixing. The origin of mixedness is itself a metrological resource variable.
\end{remark}
