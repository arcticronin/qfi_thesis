\section{Variational Quantum State Eigensolver (VQSE)} \label{sec:vqse_deep_dive}

The VQSE subroutine extracts the most important components of the eigenspace required by the TQFI bounds.
It is a quantum generalization of a Principal Component Analysis (PCA), it is bit-frugal (uses the smallest overhead of ancillary qubits between the algorithms we analyzed), but it had no implementation yet. So we implemented it for our study, and benchmarked it.


It variationally learns the $m$ largest eigenvalues $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^m$ and prepares the corresponding eigenbasis $\{|\lambda_i\rangle\}_{i=1}^m$ of an unknown $n$-qubit density matrix $\rho$~\cite{cerezo2022vqse}. In contrast, VQE targets the ground state of a known Hamiltonian.

\subsection{Mathematical Foundation: Diagonalization via Majorization}
% ==============================================================================

Rather than explicitly measuring off-diagonal matrix elements to quantify how far a state is from diagonal form, VQSE exploits the mathematical equivalence between matrix diagonalization and \textbf{quantum majorization}.

\begin{definition}[Quantum Majorization]
    Let $\rho$ be an $n$-qubit density matrix with sorted, non-increasing eigenvalues $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{2^n})$ where $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_{2^n} \ge 0$. Let $\mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (p_1, p_2, \dots, p_{2^n})$ denote the diagonal elements of the transformed state $\tilde{\rho} = V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ measured in the computational basis $\{|z_k\rangle\}$:
    \begin{equation}
        p_k(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \langle z_k | V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}) | z_k \rangle.
    \end{equation}
    By the Schur--Horn theorem, the eigenvalue vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ majorizes the diagonal probability vector $\mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$, denoted $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \succ \mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta})$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[VQSE Cost Function Minimization]
    Let $H_{\mathrm{VQSE}}$ be an $n$-qubit Hamiltonian diagonal in the computational basis with non-negative, non-degenerate eigenenergies sorted in strictly increasing order $0 \le E_1 < E_2 < \dots < E_{2^n}$. The VQSE cost function is the energy expectation value
    \begin{equation}
        C(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \operatorname{Tr}\left[ H_{\mathrm{VQSE}} V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \right] = \sum_{k=1}^{2^n} E_k p_k(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{p}(\boldsymbol{\beta}).
        \label{eq:vqse_cost_dot_product}
    \end{equation}
    Because the inner product of an increasingly sorted vector $\mathbf{E}$ with a vector $\mathbf{p}$ majorized by $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ is a Schur-concave function, $C(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ achieves its global minimum when $V(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}})$ maps the $k$-th eigenvector $|\lambda_k\rangle$ of $\rho$ to the $k$-th computational basis state $|z_k\rangle$:
    \begin{equation}
        C_{\text{min}} = \mathbf{E} \cdot \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \sum_{k=1}^{2^n} E_k \lambda_k.
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Ansatz Execution and Readout Subroutines}
% ==============================================================================

Once the hybrid quantum-classical loop converges to the optimal parameter set $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}}$, the $m$ dominant eigenvalues and eigenvectors are retrieved as follows:

1. \textbf{Eigenvalue Readout:} Prepare $V(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}}) \rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}})$ and sample in the computational basis for $N_{\text{runs}}$ shots. The estimated eigenvalue $\tilde{\lambda}_i$ corresponds to the relative frequency $f_i / N_{\text{runs}}$ of the bitstring $z_i \in \{0,1\}^n$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\lambda}_i^{\text{est}} = \frac{f_i}{N_{\text{runs}}}.
\end{equation}
2. \textbf{Eigenvector Preparation On-Demand:} Given a computational basis state $|z_i\rangle = X^{z_{i,1}} \otimes \dots \otimes X^{z_{i,n}} |0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, the corresponding approximate state eigenvector $|\tilde{\lambda}_i\rangle$ is generated on a quantum register via:
\begin{equation}
    |\tilde{\lambda}_i\rangle = V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}}) |z_i\rangle.
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{VQSE Principal Component Extraction}
    \label{alg:vqse}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Input state $\rho$, truncation rank $m$, parameterized ansatz $V(\boldsymbol{\beta})$, and non-degenerate $H_{\mathrm{VQSE}}$.
        \While{not converged}
        \State Prepare $\rho$, apply $V(\boldsymbol{\beta})$, and measure $C(\boldsymbol{\beta})$.
        \State Estimate $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}C$ and update $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ with the selected classical optimizer.
        \EndWhile
        \State Sample $V(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{opt}})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{opt}})$ in the computational basis.
        \State Estimate the $m$ dominant eigenvalues from the most frequent bitstrings and prepare their eigenvectors with $V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{opt}})$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Adaptive Cost Function:}
% ==============================================================================

The choice of the VQSE target Hamiltonian introduces a fundamental trade-off between trainability and optimization-landscape structure:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Fixed Local Hamiltonian ($H_L = \sum_j r_j Z_j$):} local cost functions, with gradients that do not exponentially vanish at shallow circuit depths, it is introduced to avoid barren plateaus. However, strict non-degeneracy restricts the solution space, it has an high density of sub-optimal local minima.
    \item \textbf{Fixed Global Hamiltonian ($H_G = \sum_{i=1}^m q_i |z_i\rangle\langle z_i|$):} Possesses a $(2^n - m)$-fold degenerate subspace, creating a large, flexible solution space. However, global cost functions exhibit exponentially vanishing gradients (barren plateaus) across random hardware-efficient Ansätze. This is the real function we ultimately aim to optimize.
\end{itemize}

As an optional optimization extension, we consider a time-dependent, adaptively updated Hamiltonian schedule that interpolates between these two costs:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{cost}}(t) = (1 - t) H_L + t H_G(t), \quad t \in [0,1],
    \label{eq:adaptive_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $t = k / N_{\text{max}}$ represents the normalized optimization progress.


As outlined in Algorithm~\ref{alg:adaptive_vqse}, $H_G(t) = \sum_{i=1}^m q_i |z_i(t)\rangle\langle z_i(t)|$ is periodically re-centered (that is why it is local) around the dominant bitstrings $z_i(t)$ sampled from intermediate measurements of $V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta})$. This dynamic shift changes the cost landscape during training. Its effectiveness should be assessed empirically for the chosen ansatz and noise model.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Adaptive VQSE Optimization Schedule}
    \label{alg:adaptive_vqse}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Input state $\rho$, rank $m$, step size $t_s = 1/s$, local $H_L$, weights $\{q_i\}_{i=1}^m$.
        \State Initialize parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}_0$ randomly; set $H_{\mathrm{cost}}(0) \leftarrow H_L$, $t \leftarrow \delta t$.
        \While{$t \le 1$}
        \If{$t$ is divisible by $t_s$}
        \State Measure $V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ in the computational basis.
        \State Identify $m$ most frequent bitstrings $Z(t) = \{z_1(t), \dots, z_m(t)\}$.
        \State Update global component: $H_G(t) \leftarrow \sum_{i=1}^m q_i |z_i(t)\rangle\langle z_i(t)|$ .
        \EndIf
        \State Update total Hamiltonian: $H_{\mathrm{cost}}(t) \leftarrow (1 - t) H_L + t H_G(t)$.
        \State Execute a classical optimization step: $\boldsymbol{\beta} \leftarrow \text{Optimizer}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} C(t, \boldsymbol{\beta}))$.
        \State $t \leftarrow t + \delta t$.
        \EndWhile
        \State \textbf{Return:} $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{opt}} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\beta}$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Finite Shot Sampling Complexity and Verification Bounds}
% ==============================================================================

Evaluating eigenvalues via empirical bitstring frequencies $f_i / N_{\text{runs}}$ introduces statistical sampling noise.

\begin{theorem}[Shot Complexity for Relative Accuracy]
    By Hoeffding's inequality, to ensure that the relative error $\epsilon_i = |\tilde{\lambda}_i^{\text{est}} - \tilde{\lambda}_i| / \tilde{\lambda}_i$ across all $m$ target eigenvalues remains below a threshold $c > 0$ with success probability at least $1 - \delta$, the required number of measurement shots scales as:
    \begin{equation}
        N_{\text{runs}} \ge \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2 c^2 \lambda_m^2} = \Omega\left( \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{c^2 \lambda_m^2} \right),
        \label{eq:shot_scaling}
    \end{equation}
    where $\lambda_m$ is the smallest eigenvalue within the target truncation rank $m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Operational Error Verification Bound]
    The total eigenvalue error $\epsilon_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^m (\lambda_i - \tilde{\lambda}_i)^2$ and eigenvector alignment error $\epsilon_v = \sum_{i=1}^m \langle \delta_i | \delta_i \rangle$ (where $|\delta_i\rangle = (\mathbb{I} - |\tilde{\lambda}_i\rangle\langle\tilde{\lambda}_i|)\rho|\tilde{\lambda}_i\rangle$) are bounded by the convergence value of the cost function $C(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{opt}})$:
    \begin{equation}
        \epsilon_\lambda, \, \epsilon_v \le \operatorname{Tr}[\rho^2] - \frac{(E_{m+1} - C(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{opt}}))^2}{\sum_{i=1}^m (E_{m+1} - E_i)^2}.
        \label{eq:operational_error_bound}
    \end{equation}
    Furthermore, an optional 2-copy verification protocol utilizing $b_m > m$ sampled bitstrings establishes an even tighter, hardware-verifiable bound:
    \begin{equation}
        \epsilon_\lambda, \, \epsilon_v \le \operatorname{Tr}[\rho^2] - \sum_{i=1}^{b_m} \tilde{\lambda}_i^2 - \frac{\left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{b_m} \tilde{\lambda}_i\right)^2}{2^n - b_m}.
        \label{eq:verification_bound_2copy}
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Comparative Resource Scaling: Why VQSE is Qubit-Frugal}
% ==============================================================================

Compared to existing quantum state diagonalization and principal component extraction algorithms, VQSE achieves ultimate qubit frugality by requiring only a \textbf{single copy of $\rho$ ($n$ qubits)} per circuit execution .

\begin{infobox}[sec:vqse_qng_optimization]{Optimizing VQSE with Quantum Natural Gradient}



    Quantum Natural Gradient descent (QNG) is an optimization technique that takes into account the local geometry of the parameter space, where the local curvature of the cost function landscape is obtained by computing the quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM) of the ansatz state.
    Quantum states are oscillating rapidly in the high-dimensional Hilbert space, and the natural gradient helps to account for this intricate geometry, leading to more efficient and stable optimization. Taking the straight Euclidean path might be inefficient \cite{Park2020_geometry_learning_neural_quantum_states}.

    \tcbline
    \faLightbulb \hspace{5pt}\textbf{In simple terms}: the QFIM
    preconditions the standard gradient. Omitting learning rate and
    regularization, the geometry-aware descent direction is
    $-F^{-1}\nabla C$
    \tcbline

    The ansatz QFIM and the general quantum natural-gradient update are defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ansatz_qfim} and~\eqref{eq:qng_update}.  In VQSE, the trainable variables are the circuit parameters $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and the objective is
    $C(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\operatorname{Tr}[H_{\mathrm{VQSE}}V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta})]$~\cite{cerezo2022vqse}.  A geometry-aware optimizer preconditions the ordinary gradient of this cost with a metric on the circuit-parameter space.  This metric concerns the training parameters and must be distinguished from the QFI for estimating the physical field $h_x$.

    This is an \enquote{inception-like} structure: QFIM of the ansatz state helps to train an algorithm that is itself used to estimate QFI \inceptiontop[0.2]~\cite{nolan2010inception}.

    Stochastic reconfiguration (SR) provides a sampled form of natural-gradient optimization for a neural quantum state~\cite{Park2020_geometry_learning_neural_quantum_states}.  For a wavefunction $\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$, define the logarithmic derivatives and their covariance matrix by
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{O}_i(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
        =\partial_{\vartheta_i}\log\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
        \qquad
        S_{ij}
        =\operatorname{Re}\!\left[
            \langle\mathcal{O}_i^*\mathcal{O}_j\rangle
            -\langle\mathcal{O}_i^*\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}_j\rangle
            \right]
        \label{eq:sr_covariance_metric}
    \end{equation}
    For a normalized pure wavefunction, $S$ represents the Fubini--Study metric up to the chosen normalization convention.  The regularized SR update is
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{k+1}
        =\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_k
        -\eta\left(S+\lambda\mathbb{I}\right)^{-1}
        \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}E
        \qquad
        \lambda\geq0
        \label{eq:sr_parameter_update}
    \end{equation}

    We tested this principle with a real, positive restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) wavefunction for the TFIM.  The implementation evaluates the logarithmic derivatives in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr_covariance_metric}, estimates $S$ and the energy gradient from Variational Monte Carlo samples, adds the diagonal regularization $\lambda\mathbb{I}$, and solves the resulting linear system.  Figure~\ref{fig:sgd_vs_ng} compares SR with ordinary stochastic gradient descent in this tractable pure-state example.

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{misc/sgd_vs_sr_comparison-set2-palette.png}
        \caption{Energy minimization for an $L=8$  TFIM at $g=1=\frac{h_x}{J}$ using an RBM neural quantum state~\cite{carleo2017nqs}.  Both optimizers use $80$ steps, $300$ Monte Carlo samples per step, a learning rate of $0.05$, and the same random seed.  SR uses the diagonal regularization $\lambda=10^{-4}$.  The left panel shows the estimated energy, and the right panel shows its absolute error from the exact ground-state energy on a logarithmic scale.  In this run, SR reaches a lower error than stochastic gradient descent, and also converges it faster. $L$ indicates the complexity of the ansatz, as~\eqref{def:lhea}. In this RBM scenario it indicates the factor of the number of hidden units relative to the number of visible units.}
        \label{fig:sgd_vs_ng}
    \end{figure}

    Applying the same idea to VQSE requires care because $V(\boldsymbol{\beta})\rho V^\dagger(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ remains mixed when the input $\rho$ is mixed.  The exact intrinsic metric is then the mixed-state ansatz QFIM, while a pure-state Fubini--Study or SR metric is a surrogate preconditioner unless we use a suitable purification or mixed-state estimator.  Anyway, the approach remains viable, but it becomes slow for a highly parameterized circuit.  A full metric for $N_{\mathrm{par}}$ parameters contains $\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{par}}^2)$ elements, and solving the regularized update adds a substantial classical cost~\cite{kolotouros2024RNG,gacon2021_perturbation_stochastic_qfi}.
\end{infobox}
